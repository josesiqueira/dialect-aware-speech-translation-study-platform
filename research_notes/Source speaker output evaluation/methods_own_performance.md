# Established methods in which participants evaluate records of their own speech or performance (grounding for the proposed "show participants the AI output of their own recordings" step)

Scope note. The proposed step: after recording spontaneous Finnish speech (no AI output shown) and answering a background questionnaire, each participant sees the Finnish ASR transcript and two English translations (default prompt vs preservation-aware prompt) of their own recordings, possibly hears synthesized English audio, and answers a second questionnaire ("does this represent what I meant and how I speak"). All sources below were checked on a real page (Crossref, PubMed, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, ACL Anthology, publisher or journal page, or full text). Where only an abstract was read, this is stated. Books and preprints are labelled.

## 1. Stimulated recall and retrospective verbal reports

### Takeaway
Stimulated recall is a well-established, peer-reviewed family of methods in which participants are shown or played a record of their own performance and comment on it; its literature recommends a minimal delay between event and recall, names veridicality and reactivity as the main validity threats, and has experimental evidence that a recall session placed after a task need not change later performance. The proposed step borrows the stimulated recall setup (own recording as the stimulus, same session) but asks participants to judge an artefact (the AI output) rather than to report their past thoughts, so it is a variant, not a textbook application.

### Cited Findings
- Definition and core design: stimulated recall (SR) is "a family of introspective research procedures through which cognitive processes can be investigated by inviting subjects to recall, when prompted by a video sequence, their concurrent thinking during that event" (abstract). Source: Lyle (2003), British Educational Research Journal 29(6), 861-878 ([DOI 10.1080/0141192032000137349](https://doi.org/10.1080/0141192032000137349)).
- Validity threats named by Lyle: subjects may reorder their accounts "in response to activating deeper memory structures and in order to maintain biases of control and a priori theory affirmation"; value is enhanced by "immediacy of recall, consonance between questions and cognitive organisation, and indirect means of introspection" (abstract). Source: [Lyle 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/0141192032000137349).
- The standard SLA methods book (BOOK, not a journal article): Gass and Mackey, *Stimulated Recall Methodology in Second Language Research* (Erlbaum, 2000; Routledge reissue record [DOI 10.4324/9781410606006](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781410606006)); second edition *Stimulated Recall Methodology in Applied Linguistics and L2 Research* (Routledge; Crossref date 2016, [DOI 10.4324/9781315813349](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315813349)). Book existence and titles verified on Crossref; contents below come from a published review, not from the book itself.
- According to the LINGUIST List review of Gass and Mackey (2000): stimuli may be visual or oral (video or audio recordings) or other tangible reminders such as drafts of a composition; either participant or researcher may pause the recording; the authors repeatedly stress that "the time interval between the event and the recall must be a minimum", and the reviewer lists running the recall session immediately after the event as a way to reduce limitations; listed disadvantages include memory limits over long intervals, "veridictability" (veridicality), "Generating verbal reports may alter the nature of the process" (reactivity), and inaccurate reporting by participants; the book gives two sample protocols, coding sheets and rater-training guidance, and advises raters independent of the original event. Source: [LINGUIST List 11.1414 review](https://linguistlist.org/issues/11/1414/) (a book review, secondary).
- Peer-reviewed SSLA example of the canonical procedure with native speakers reviewing their own interaction: "Immediately after the task, each NS participated in a stimulated recall, viewing a videotape of the interaction and commenting on the interaction"; SR findings were used to corroborate quantitative interaction measures. Source: Polio, Gass and Chapin (2006), Studies in Second Language Acquisition 28(2), 237-267 ([DOI 10.1017/S0272263106060116](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263106060116)) (abstract read).
- Reactivity tested experimentally: 44 learners of Japanese assigned to stimulated recall, stimulus-only (viewed own video, no recall), experimental control and test control; SR took place after communicative activities and before post-tests; "non-significant differences between these three groups, suggesting the non-reactivity of stimulated recall, stimuli and verbalisation", with the caveat that reactivity "cannot be discussed independently of various factors, such as the nature of stimuli, type of verbalisation and learner-interviewer interaction" (abstract). Source: Egi (2008), Language Awareness 17(3), 212-228 ([DOI 10.1080/09658410802146859](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658410802146859)).
- Meta-analysis on reactivity of verbal reports (94 studies, about 3,500 participants): plain think-aloud effect on performance "indistinguishable from zero (r = -.03)", but "procedures that entail describing or explaining thoughts and actions are significantly reactive, leading to higher performance than silent control conditions"; all verbal reporting increases task time (abstract). Source: Fox, Ericsson and Best (2011), Psychological Bulletin 137(2), 316-344 ([DOI 10.1037/a0021663](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021663); [PubMed 21090887](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21090887/)).
- Foundational theory of verbal reports: Ericsson and Simon (1980), "Verbal reports as data", Psychological Review 87(3), 215-251 ([DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.87.3.215](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.87.3.215)); classic critique that people often cannot accurately report on their own mental processes: Nisbett and Wilson (1977), "Telling more than we can know", Psychological Review 84(3), 231-259 ([DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231)). (Bibliographic records verified on Crossref; content characterisation of Ericsson and Simon is as summarised in Fox et al. 2011's abstract.)
- Use outside SLA: stimulated recall interviews in ethnography "involves interviewing individuals by playing them audio or audiovisual recordings of their own behavior in social situations and discussing different aspects of those recorded interactions", to learn "what signals interactants understand as important, what signals they try to convey to others" (publisher abstract). Source: Dempsey (2010), Qualitative Sociology 33(3), 349-367 ([DOI 10.1007/s11133-010-9157-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11133-010-9157-x)).

### Inferences
- The proposed step matches stimulated recall on three structural points: the participant's own recording is the stimulus, the session comes after the task, and it is in the same sitting (consistent with the "minimum interval" and "immediacy" advice in Gass and Mackey via the review, Lyle 2003, and Polio et al. 2006).
- It differs on the object of judgment. Classic SR asks "what were you thinking at that moment"; the proposed step asks "does this AI artefact represent what you meant and how you speak". The "what I meant" item is closest to SR (it relies on recall of intent), so the veridicality threat applies to it directly; the "how I speak" item is a perception judgment (see section 4), so SR literature alone does not ground it.
- Fox et al. (2011) imply that items asking participants to explain or justify (for example, "why is this translation wrong?") are more reactive than simple ratings. Since the evaluation comes after all speech is collected, this reactivity cannot affect the speech data; it can affect later items within the evaluation questionnaire itself (order effects between items), which is a design point, not a fatal flaw.
- Recommended practice transferable from SR (inference from the cited guidance): play the participant's own audio alongside the AI output so judgments of "what I meant" are anchored to the record, not to unaided memory; use fixed, non-leading prompts; pilot the protocol; and treat answers as perceptions, not ground truth.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Gass and Mackey book chapters directly, so specific numeric timing advice (a commonly repeated "within 48 hours" figure) is NOT verified and should not be cited without checking the book.
- I attempted to verify Mackey, Gass and McDonough (2000) "How do learners perceive interactional feedback?" (SSLA) but the DOI I tried resolved to a different article (DeKeyser 2000); not cited.

## 2. Member checking, respondent validation and interviewee transcript review

### Takeaway
Returning data or interpretations to participants for checking is an established qualitative practice (member checking, respondent validation, interviewee transcript review), but the peer-reviewed methodological literature is sceptical that it improves accuracy, and documents side effects (participants editing or deleting content, embarrassment at seeing their own speech in writing). Its strongest justification is when the research question is itself about representation, which fits the proposed step.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "Member checking, also known as participant or respondent validation, is a technique for exploring the credibility of results. Data or results are returned to participants to check for accuracy and resonance with their experiences"; the authors critique simplistic reporting of it as one item in a list of validation techniques and describe "Synthesized Member Checking", in which participants engage with and add to interview and interpreted data "several months after their semi-structured interview" (abstract). Source: Birt, Scott, Cavers, Campbell and Walter (2016), Qualitative Health Research 26(13), 1802-1811 ([DOI 10.1177/1049732316654870](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732316654870); [PubMed 27340178](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27340178/)).
- Review of published member checks: four underlying purposes identified (theory generalisation, representation, participation, change); member checks "are unlikely to be relevant to research focussed on theory development and generalisation"; "there was little evidence that member checks improved research findings"; they are useful for participant approval of quotations and where anonymity cannot be guaranteed (abstract). Source: Thomas (2017), Qualitative Research in Psychology 14(1), 23-41 ([DOI 10.1080/14780887.2016.1219435](https://doi.org/10.1080/14780887.2016.1219435)).
- Interviewee transcript review (ITR) tracked across 51 interviews: ITR "added little to the accuracy of the transcript and may create complications if the goal of the researcher is to produce a transcript which reflects precisely what was said"; interviewees made corrections and clarifications, sometimes added new material; disadvantages include "a bias created by inconsistent data sources or the loss of data when an interviewee chooses to remove valuable material" (abstract). Source: Hagens, Dobrow and Chafe (2009), BMC Medical Research Methodology 9:47 ([DOI 10.1186/1471-2288-9-47](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-9-47)).
- Participants reading their own transcripts: responses included ratification, "corrections of language", clarifications, shifts in power balance, and "feelings of embarrassment and threat"; the author calls for "added caution and consideration when sending transcripts to interviewees" (abstract). Source: Mero-Jaffe (2011), "'Is that what I said?' Interview transcript approval by participants", International Journal of Qualitative Methods 10(3), 231-247 ([DOI 10.1177/160940691101000304](https://doi.org/10.1177/160940691101000304)).
- Critical reflection on sharing transcripts: considers "the relationship between the transcript and the representation (re-presentation) of what participants believe to have occurred within the interview" and its ethical implications (abstract). Source: Forbat and Henderson (2005), Qualitative Health Research 15(8), 1114-1128 ([DOI 10.1177/1049732305279065](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732305279065); [PubMed 16221883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16221883/)).
- Motulsky (2021), "Is member checking the gold standard of quality in qualitative research?", Qualitative Psychology 8(3), 389-406 ([DOI 10.1037/qup0000215](https://doi.org/10.1037/qup0000215)). Bibliographic record verified on Crossref; I could not read the abstract. Secondary sources found in search cite it for burdens on researchers and participants and for risk of participant embarrassment or pain; treat this characterisation as secondary.

### Inferences
- The closest member-checking analogue is interviewee transcript review: the participant sees a written rendering of their own speech (here produced by ASR, not a human transcriber) and judges its fidelity. Mero-Jaffe's finding that people "correct the language" of their own verbatim transcripts is directly relevant: Finnish speakers seeing colloquial or dialect forms written out may judge a standardised ASR transcript as "better" even when it erases their dialect. This is a predictable bias for a "does this represent how I speak" item, and should be stated as a limitation or controlled (for example, by asking separately "is this accurate" and "do you like how it reads").
- Hagens et al. and Thomas argue against using participant review to replace the data of record. In the proposed design the recordings remain the data of record and the evaluation is a separate dataset; that is consistent with this literature, provided participants' comments are not used to edit the recordings or reference transcripts.
- Thomas's "representation" purpose is the one under which member checks are most justified. The proposed step is explicitly about whether AI output represents the speaker, so it falls within the defensible use, not the "validate the researcher's theory" use that Thomas finds weakly supported.
- Embarrassment risk (Mero-Jaffe; Motulsky as cited secondarily) supports adding a debrief and an opt-out for the evaluation stage in the ethics plan.

### Gaps
- Lincoln and Guba (1985), *Naturalistic Inquiry* (BOOK), is widely cited as the origin of member checks as a credibility technique, but I did not verify a page or quotation in this session; do not quote it without checking.
- I did not read Birt et al. (2016) full text, so details of the Synthesized Member Checking procedure beyond the abstract are not reported.

## 3. Source-speaker or self-evaluation of ASR, MT or speech translation of one's own speech

### Takeaway
There is peer-reviewed precedent for people evaluating AI renderings of their own voice or speech (a FAccT 2025 study in which speakers rated AI clones of their own voices for accent fidelity; HCI studies of how ASR failures on one's own speech affect users), and for source-language authors judging machine translations of their own text into a language they do not know. I found no peer-reviewed study in which source speakers rated speech translations of their own spontaneous speech, so the proposed step combines established components in a configuration I could not find documented.

### Cited Findings
- Own-voice clone evaluation (closest precedent found): Michel, Kaur, Gillespie, Gleason, Wilson and Ghosh (2025), "'It's not a representation of me': Examining Accent Bias and Digital Exclusion in Synthetic AI Voice Services", Proceedings of FAccT 2025, 228-245 ([DOI 10.1145/3715275.3732018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3715275.3732018); full text read at [arXiv 2504.09346](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1)). Procedure (sections 4.3, 6): 26 interview participants self-identified with one of five English accents; each read a one-minute passage; the team built a clone per participant on Speechify and ElevenLabs; participants heard their original recording first, then each labelled clone, and after each clip rated Accent Mimicry Quality (5-point scale covering intonation, pronunciation, pitch and speed; Cronbach's alpha 0.883) and how accurately the service reproduced their voice (1 to 100%), followed by questions on emotional responses and concerns. Separate survey sample N = 250 (clones built from Speech Accent Archive recordings, not own voices).
- Findings from Michel et al. (2025): participants said Speechify clones "neutralized" their accent, gave a wrong accent or no accent (P2: "That was rubbish…I'm not even going to say [it was] me"); some said ElevenLabs over-emphasised their accent or "put [it] through a filter"; eight said the ElevenLabs clone was indistinguishable from their recording. Stated limitation: reading a standardised passage "may not capture natural speech patterns or dialect variation". No participant reported distress. Source: [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1).
- Source authors judging MT into a language they do not know ("outbound translation"): tested backward translation, quality estimation with alignment, and source paraphrasing as feedback (English to Czech and Estonian); backward-translation feedback "has a mixed effect on the whole process": it raises user confidence but does not improve objective translation quality. Source: Zouhar et al. (2021), NAACL 2021, 151-161 ([ACL Anthology 2021.naacl-main.14](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.14/); [DOI 10.18653/v1/2021.naacl-main.14](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.naacl-main.14)).
- Users experiencing ASR on their own speech: in a controlled experiment (N = 108) Black and white participants interacted with a voice assistant programmed with high vs low error rates; Black participants in the high-error condition showed "significantly higher levels of self-consciousness, lower levels of self-esteem and positive affect, and less favorable ratings of the technology"; white participants did not show this pattern (abstract). Source: Wenzel, Devireddy, Davison and Kaufman (2023), CHI 2023, 1-14 ([DOI 10.1145/3544548.3581357](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581357)).
- Diary study of ASR errors on African American speakers' own speech: errors "surface thoughts about identity, namely about race and geographic location", participants feel "othered", and "African Americans accommodate their speech to have better success with the technology"; the authors argue diary studies suit communities "often misunderstood by ASR" (abstract). Source: Mengesha, Heldreth, Lahav, Sublewski and Tuennerman (2021), Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence 4 ([DOI 10.3389/frai.2021.725911](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2021.725911)).
- Black older adults using a voice assistant for health information: study identified communication breakdowns and repair types and "cultural code-switching that has to be done by these older adults when interacting with voice assistants" (abstract). Source: Harrington, Garg, Woodward and Williams (2022), CHI 2022, 1-15 ([DOI 10.1145/3491102.3501995](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3501995)).

### Inferences
- Michel et al. (2025) give a directly reusable, peer-reviewed procedure for the synthesized-audio part: play the participant's original first, then the labelled AI version, then collect a multi-item fidelity scale plus an overall accuracy rating and open questions. Their quotation "It's not a representation of me" is the same construct the proposed second questionnaire targets.
- Zouhar et al. (2021) is a warning for the English-translation part: people judging a translation of their own words into a language they do not fully command become more confident without becoming more accurate. Participants' ratings of the English translations should therefore be treated as a measure of perceived representation and acceptability, analysed against their English proficiency, and paired with an independent bilingual or expert assessment if translation accuracy is the claim.
- Mengesha et al. (2021) and Wenzel et al. (2023) show that seeing AI fail on one's own speech has affective consequences and can change how people speak. That supports both the ethics plan (debrief) and the ordering argument in section 5.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found in which source speakers rated MT or speech-to-speech translation of their own spontaneous speech. Expressive speech-to-speech translation evaluations that surfaced in search (for example the Seamless report, and a human evaluation protocol for expressive S2ST at [arXiv 2301.10606](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.10606), a PREPRINT) appear, from search snippets only, to use bilingual third-party annotators rather than the original speakers; I did not read them in full.
- A preprint on AAC users and AI-generated voices and cultural or linguistic identity ([arXiv 2605.24337](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24337)) appeared in search but was not read; status unknown.
- No peer-reviewed study found in which speakers reviewed ASR transcripts of their own dialectal speech and rated accuracy; searches returned only listener-based or reference-based ASR evaluations.

## 4. Perceptual dialectology, folk linguistics and self-perception of one's own dialect and voice

### Takeaway
Folk linguistics (including the Finnish tradition of Mielikäinen and Palander) routinely asks speakers about their own dialect and its features, so asking Finnish participants whether output "sounds like how I speak" has disciplinary precedent; but sociolinguistic evidence shows conscious self-reports of one's own usage are often inaccurate and shaped by prestige and stigma, and own-voice research shows a self-enhancement bias and large individual differences in what sounds like "my voice". Such judgments are valid as data about perception and identity, not as measurements of actual dialect features.

### Cited Findings
- Inaccurate self-evaluation: "Favourable attitudes to non-standard speech are not normally expressed, however, and emerge only in inaccurate self-evaluation test responses" (Norwich, working-class men over-reporting non-standard forms) (abstract). Source: Trudgill (1972), Language in Society 1(2), 179-195 ([DOI 10.1017/S0047404500000488](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0047404500000488)).
- Labov's New York study (BOOK chapter "Self-evaluation and linguistic security" in *The Social Stratification of English in New York City*, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press): a search result quoting the chapter states that "in the conscious report of their own usage, however, New York respondents are very inaccurate", while their reactions to others' speech were consistent. Source: [Cambridge book part record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/CBO9780511618208A022/type/BOOK_PART) (quotation seen via search summary, chapter not read in full; verify before quoting).
- Methodological principle: "Observations of the vernacular provide the most systematic basis for linguistic theory, but have been the most difficult kinds of data for linguists to obtain"; intersubjective agreement "is best reached by convergence of several kinds of data with complementary sources of error" (abstract). Source: Labov (1972), "Some principles of linguistic methodology", Language in Society 1(1), 97-120 ([DOI 10.1017/s0047404500006576](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0047404500006576)).
- Perceptual dialectology on familiarity (Preston, as reported by Palander): already in his first folk-linguistic studies in the 1980s Preston found that nearby dialects are distinguished and recognised more precisely than geographically distant ones, which Palander attributes to familiarity (Preston 1989, *Perceptual Dialectology*, BOOK, pp. 35-38, cited secondarily). Source: Palander (2007), "Alueellisen taustan vaikutus murrekäsityksiin", Virittäjä 1/2007, 24-55 ([full text PDF](https://journal.fi/virittaja/article/download/40541/9967/28049)).
- Finnish speakers' views of their own dialect (Palander 2007, East Finland and Helsinki-region youth aged 20 to 30, questionnaire interviews 1998 to 2004): one 2002 questionnaire asked "puhuvatko nuoret murretta, mitä murretta he puhuvat ja millaisia murteellisuuksia itse käyttävät" ("whether the young people speak dialect, which dialect they speak and which dialect features they themselves use"); North Karelians are aware of their dialect's "leveys" (broadness) and drawling and do not find these features aesthetic, they carry a rural stigma, partly because outsiders, especially southern Finns, have remarked on the dialect; South Karelians consider their dialect "neutraalia" and "lähempänä yleiskieltä" (neutral, closer to standard Finnish). Source: [Palander 2007, Virittäjä](https://journal.fi/virittaja/article/download/40541/9967/28049).
- Finnish dialect attitudes and self-perception: Finns describe regionally neutral speech, "varsinkin omaa puhekieltään" (especially their own colloquial speech), as "sekamurre" (mixed dialect); attitudes to one's own language are usually "kahtalainen" (ambivalent), explained by self-criticism and a conflict between status and solidarity, and are linked to speakers' linguistic self-esteem; in a 2001 questionnaire, upper-secondary students in Reisjärvi estimated that assimilated vowel variants are even more common in their own colloquial speech than in the local dialect (Hylkilä 2001, cited). Source: Mielikäinen and Palander (2002), "Suomalaisten murreasenteista", Sananjalka 44 ([DOI 10.30673/sja.86635](https://journal.fi/sananjalka/article/view/86635); full text read).
- Finnish folk-linguistic metalanguage (BOOK): Mielikäinen and Palander (2014), *Miten suomalaiset puhuvat murteista? Kansanlingvistinen tutkimus metakielestä*, Suomi 203, SKS, 260 pp., ISBN 978-952-222-538-2; studies how laypeople name and describe varieties and which dialect features they recognise. Source: review in Virittäjä 4/2015, p. 604 ([PDF](https://journal.fi/virittaja/article/download/50882/16821/51569)); open copy listed at [UEF eRepo](https://erepo.uef.fi/handle/123456789/24791).
- Own-voice self-enhancement: 80 participants, not told their own recordings were included, "rated their own voices as sounding more attractive than others had rated their voices" and more attractive than they rated others' voices ("vocal implicit egotism") (abstract). Source: Hughes and Harrison (2013), Perception 42(9), 941-949 ([DOI 10.1068/p7526](https://doi.org/10.1068/p7526); [PubMed 24386714](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24386714/)).
- Self-enhancement extends to modified self-voice: participants rated their own recordings as more attractive than others did, and loudness-modified versions of their voice as more attractive and similar than pitch-shifted versions; the bias "can be generalised to similar and familiar versions of self-voice" (abstract). Source: Peng, Wang, Meng, Liu and Hu (2019), Australian Journal of Psychology 71(3), 212-222 ([DOI 10.1111/ajpy.12235](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajpy.12235)).
- Recorded own voice is perceived differently from the spoken voice; "there is no general filter that can represent own voice for everyone", with large individual differences in which recording sounds most like one's own voice (abstract). Source: Kimura and Yotsumoto (2018), PLOS ONE 13(6), e0199443 ([DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0199443](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0199443)).
- Classic source on the voice as a self-percept: Holzman and Rousey (1966), "The voice as a percept", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 4(1), 79-86 ([DOI 10.1037/h0023518](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023518)) (record verified; content not read).

### Inferences
- Speakers can make meaningful judgments about whether output "sounds like me" or "erases my dialect" (Finnish folk linguistics routinely elicits such views, and Michel et al. 2025 show speakers detect neutralised accents), but they are unreliable reporters of which specific features they use (Trudgill 1972; Labov as quoted). A defensible design asks about perceived representation and identity, and measures actual dialect-feature preservation separately (for example, by annotating the recordings and outputs), which is Labov's "convergence of several kinds of data with complementary sources of error".
- The direction of bias is predictable from the Finnish sources: speakers of stigmatised varieties (the North Karelian example) may welcome standardisation in the output, while speakers with strong dialect identity may penalise it. Background-questionnaire items on dialect identity and attitudes therefore become useful covariates for the evaluation ratings.
- For synthesized English audio, self-voice ratings are biased (self-enhancement, Hughes and Harrison 2013; Peng et al. 2019) and noisy (Kimura and Yotsumoto 2018). Ratings of a cloned voice as "me" should be interpreted relative to the participant's rating of their own unprocessed recording (the Michel et al. order: original first, then clone), not as absolute scores.
- Assumption: Finnish participants hearing an English synthesized voice are judging a cross-language rendering; none of the own-voice studies found tested cross-language self-recognition, so transfer of these findings to that case is an assumption.

### Gaps
- I did not find a peer-reviewed Finnish study in which speakers listened to their own recorded speech and evaluated how dialectal it is; the Finnish sources found use questionnaires and interviews about one's dialect, not playback of one's own recordings.
- Preston's own books (1989; *Handbook of Perceptual Dialectology* 1999) were not read; Preston findings here are as reported by Palander (2007).
- Social desirability in own-dialect self-report is implied by the prestige and stigma findings but I found no Finnish study quantifying it.

## 5. Ordering: collect speech first, reveal AI output later, then evaluate

### Takeaway
The order "elicit speech without feedback, then show AI output, then evaluate" follows standard practice in stimulated recall (recall after the task) and is supported by evidence that exposure to ASR errors leads speakers to change how they speak and affects their self-perception; I found no source that names this order as a formal contamination-control procedure, so the rationale is an inference from established findings rather than a single cited rule.

### Cited Findings
- In canonical SR studies the recall follows the task: "Immediately after the task, each NS participated in a stimulated recall". Source: [Polio, Gass and Chapin 2006](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263106060116).
- SR placed after communicative tasks and before post-tests did not significantly change post-test results (no reactivity found, with caveats on stimulus type and interaction). Source: [Egi 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658410802146859).
- Procedures that require describing or explaining one's thinking are reactive (change performance), whereas plain think-aloud is not. Source: [Fox, Ericsson and Best 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021663).
- After experiencing ASR errors on their own speech, African American users "accommodate their speech to have better success with the technology". Source: [Mengesha et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2021.725911). Related: "cultural code-switching" when interacting with voice assistants. Source: [Harrington et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3501995).
- Exposure to high ASR error rates raised self-consciousness and lowered self-esteem and positive affect for Black participants. Source: [Wenzel et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581357).
- The vernacular is the most valuable and hardest kind of data to obtain. Source: [Labov 1972](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0047404500006576).
- Interviewee review of their own transcripts can lead to removal or revision of content, creating inconsistent data sources. Source: [Hagens et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-9-47).

### Inferences
- Speech accommodation to AI (Mengesha et al.; Harrington et al.) means that showing participants the ASR or translation of their speech before or between recording tasks could lead them to standardise their Finnish in later tasks, which would contaminate exactly the dialectal and colloquial data the study depends on. Placing the reveal after all speech is recorded removes this route.
- Placing the background questionnaire before the reveal also protects self-reported dialect identity and attitude answers from being shifted by seeing the AI output (inference from Wenzel et al.'s affect findings and the prestige effects in section 4); this is an assumption, not a tested result for this population.
- Keeping the recordings as the data of record and the evaluation as a separate dataset avoids the "inconsistent data sources" problem that Hagens et al. report for transcript review.
- A residual threat that ordering does not remove: within the evaluation, the first artefact seen (for example, the default translation) can anchor judgments of the second. Counterbalancing the order of the default and preservation-aware outputs across participants, and blinding which prompt produced which output, are standard remedies (my inference; no source in this set tests it for this task).
- Overall assessment (inference): the step is not an invented method.
