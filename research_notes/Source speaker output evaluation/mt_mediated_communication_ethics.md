# User experience of MT, ASR and speech translation of one's own speech, and the ethics of showing participants AI output about their speech

Scope note: these notes cover (a) how people experience and judge machine translation (MT), automatic speech recognition (ASR) and speech translation of what they themselves said, and (b) ethical guidance and risks of showing participants AI output that may misrecognize, standardize or stereotype their speech, including Finnish research ethics (TENK) and GDPR. Every cited claim was checked on the page linked (full text read unless marked "abstract only" or "search summary only"). Inferences are separated and labelled. Format: claim, then (Source).

## Q1. HCI/CSCW studies of MT-mediated and speech-translation-mediated communication: what users noticed, how they judged translations of their own words, trust and inability to verify

### Takeaway
Lab studies consistently find that users cannot reliably judge translations of their own words into a language they do not know, so they rely on misleading proxies (fluency, conversation flow), adapt their own speech to the machine (slower, simpler, clearer), and sometimes misattribute machine errors to the other speaker. Seeing one's own ASR transcript is the main thing that lets speakers notice errors, and it is also when they report frustration at being misrepresented.

### Cited Findings
- Hara and Iqbal (CHI 2015, "Effect of Machine Translation in Interlingual Conversation: Lessons from a Formative Study", DOI 10.1145/2702123.2702407): formative lab study with 23 pairs (46 participants) doing storytelling tasks through a prototype spoken language translation app (ASR, then MT, then text-to-speech), under three interface settings: closed caption (CC), text-to-speech (TTS), and both. Language groups were French-German and English-German. (Source: [Hara and Iqbal 2015, author PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf))
- Hara and Iqbal: 84.8% of participants said they changed the way they spoke to accommodate the system (slowing down, enunciating, speaking louder, simpler words and sentence structure). One English speaker: "we all have our own slung [slang] and we have a tendency to run words together, and software may not pick up". Speaking too slowly sometimes backfired because ASR treated pauses as sentence ends. (Source: [Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf))
- Hara and Iqbal: a transcript of the participant's own utterance (closed caption) let 43.5% of participants see whether the system recognized them and adjust how they spoke. Participants' self-rated ASR quality rose across rounds, which the authors read as speakers adapting their speech to the system, while actual transcription "was far from perfect". (Source: [Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf))
- Hara and Iqbal: seeing one's own misrecognition without being able to stop it was a reported experience: "I was seeing my own recognition in French, I started laughing because what was going to be sent was wrong. I saw very bad recognition. I knew I couldn't do anything". More than half (56.5%) reported proper nouns (names) not being recognized, which repeating or rephrasing could not fix; 23.9% reported idiomatic and colloquial expressions translated literally ("it conveys the literal meaning but not the cultural one"). About a third (32.6%) said they did not feel uncomfortable adapting "because it was expected". (Source: [Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf))
- Hara and Iqbal: hearing the partner's original voice alongside the synthetic translation was valued by 32.6%: "if you only get the robotic voice, I think it just cuts you off from the person you are engaged with". (Source: [Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf))
- Robertson and Díaz (FAccT 2022, "Understanding and Being Understood: User Strategies for Identifying and Recovering From Mistranslations in Machine Translation-Mediated Chat", DOI 10.1145/3531146.3534638): 19 MT-mediated role-play chats (housing and employment scenarios) with English paired with Spanish, Farsi, Igbo or Tagalog, followed by in-depth debrief interviews. Users "broadly lacked relevant and helpful information to guide their assessments of translation quality"; cases of falsely believing they had understood were rare but potentially serious. (Source: [Robertson and Díaz 2022, FAccT PDF](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf))
- Robertson and Díaz: participants used fluency and conversation flow as a "(misleading) proxy for adequacy"; some felt "a hundred percent" confident without any verification. Perceptions were asymmetric: one participant receiving better translations did not realize "how poorly their own messages were translated", which hindered repair. Igbo and Farsi speakers showed higher tolerance for errors, informed by past negative MT experiences in their languages. (Source: [Robertson and Díaz 2022](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf))
- Robertson and Díaz: disfluent output had social consequences because it was hard to tell whether an odd message reflected the other person's intentions or was an MT artifact. Self-repair (fixing one's own message before sending, e.g. via back-translation) is "challenging for users who do not speak the target language", since they must guess whether a fix will work. (Source: [Robertson and Díaz 2022](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf))
- Gao, Xu, Cosley and Fussell (CSCW 2014, "How beliefs about the presence of machine translation impact multilingual collaborations"): in MT-mediated collaboration the presence of MT is not obvious, because poor English can come from either a partner's limited fluency or MT errors; in a lab experiment crossing told-MT-present/absent with actual MT use, beliefs about MT presence significantly affected the collaboration experience. (Abstract only, via search summary of a hosted copy; Source: [Cornell publications list](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~danco/research/pubs.htm))
- Gao, Wang, Cosley and Fussell (CHI 2013, "Same translation but different experience: the effects of highlighting on machine-translated conversations", DOI 10.1145/2470654.2470719): lab experiment with native English speakers interacting with a Mandarin-speaking confederate via MT, testing whether keyword highlighting helps people overlook errors. (Search summary only; DOI confirmed in Robertson and Díaz's reference list; Source: [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2470654.2470719))
- Mehandru, Robertson and Salehi (FAccT 2022, "Reliable and Safe Use of Machine Translation in Medical Settings", DOI 10.1145/3531146.3533244): interviews with 20 clinicians; "even small mistranslations can have life-threatening consequences"; clinicians cope with back-translation, non-verbal communication and testing patient understanding, and face accountability problems when miscommunication occurs. (Source: [Mehandru et al. 2022, FAccT PDF](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3533244.pdf))
- Liebling et al. (CHI 2020, "Unmet Needs and Opportunities for Mobile Translation AI", DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376260): three studies (US travelers, migrant workers in India, immigrants in the US); the low-proficiency groups have the greatest translation needs, which current mobile translation apps do not meet. (Abstract only; Source: [arXiv 2002.12387](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.12387); [Google Research page](https://research.google/pubs/unmet-needs-and-opportunities-for-mobile-translation-ai/))
- Yamashita and Ishida (CSCW 2006, "Effects of machine translation on collaborative work", DOI 10.1145/1180875.1180955) exists with this title, authors and venue. (Bibliographic record only; Source: [Semantic Scholar API record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/1180875.1180955?fields=title,authors,venue,year,abstract,externalIds))

### Inferences
- Inference: in the planned study, Finnish speakers evaluating English output of their own speech face exactly the verification problem Robertson and Díaz describe. Their judgment of translation adequacy will be weakest where English proficiency is lowest, and they may use English fluency as a proxy for accuracy. Their judgments of the Finnish ASR transcript (their own language) should be much more reliable than their judgments of the English translation.
- Inference: Hara and Iqbal show that self-rated ASR quality can rise because speakers adapt, not because the system improves. If participants interact with the system during the session before the end-of-session review, their speech may drift toward standard Finnish, which would both confound dialect analysis and be a finding in itself.
- Inference: the "original voice plus synthetic voice" finding suggests presenting the synthetic English voice alongside the participant's original recording, not alone.

### Gaps
- Could not read the Yamashita and Ishida 2006 abstract or findings (ACM page returned 403; Semantic Scholar has no abstract). Its findings are not reported here.
- No peer-reviewed study was found (within this search budget) of speech-to-speech translation apps in real health care or travel use where speakers evaluated translations of their own speech; Mehandru et al. interviewed clinicians, not patients. A EUSEM 2017 case study on speech translators with Arabic-speaking standardized patients is cited in Mehandru et al. but was not checked.
- No study found on Finnish speakers' experience of MT or speech translation of their own speech.

## Q2. Speakers reacting to ASR or voice technology misrepresenting their accent or dialect: harms reported and research designs used

### Takeaway
Three well-documented studies (Mengesha et al. 2021; Harrington et al. 2022; Michel et al. 2025) show that when voice technology misrecognizes or misrepresents a speaker's dialect or accent, people report frustration, feeling "othered", identity-related thoughts (race, region), self-blame, and pressure to code-switch toward a standard variety. Michel et al. is the closest design match: participants heard AI clones of their own voice and rated them, and reported that neutralized accents were "not a representation of me".

### Cited Findings
- Mengesha, Heldreth, Lahav, Sublewski and Tuennerman (2021), "'I don't Think These Devices are Very Culturally Sensitive.' Impact of Automated Speech Recognition Errors on African Americans", Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence 4, DOI 10.3389/frai.2021.725911 (peer-reviewed). Design: two-week diary study with 30 African American participants in several US cities, screened from 1,865 survey respondents, with daily entries, reflections on negative experiences, screen recordings of failures, and assigned tasks; thematic coding of open and closed responses and video transcripts. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002))
- Mengesha et al.: errors commonly caused frustration (77%), feeling bothered (58%), disappointment (55%), anger (52%) and anxiety (36%), and prompted thoughts about racial identity and about regional or location identity (20% each). Participants felt "othered"; one said the technology is "made for the standard middle-aged white American, which I am not". 17% blamed themselves for how they spoke. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002))
- Mengesha et al.: 93% (28 of 30) reported modifying their speech (slower, enunciating, avoiding slang); accommodation itself caused negative emotions (bothered 67%, frustration 53%, self-consciousness 17%). The authors recommend studying how African American users want their speech transcribed and involving community members (community-based participatory research). They report the diary study as a useful method for communities often misunderstood by ASR. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002); [Frontiers article page](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2021.725911/full))
- Harrington, Garg, Woodward and Williams (CHI 2022), "'It's Kind of Like Code-Switching': Black Older Adults' Experiences with a Voice Assistant for Health Information Seeking", DOI 10.1145/3491102.3501995. Design: three-phase qualitative study with 30 Black older adults (aged 50 to 89) in Chicago and Detroit: five-day diary of health questions, semi-structured interviews, one-on-one testing with Google Home. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9307214/))
- Harrington et al.: participants described adjusting speech, vocabulary and tone and dropping informal or AAVE-influenced expressions to be understood ("It's kind of like code-switching", P11); this was mentally demanding, some abandoned questions, some felt the device was not made for them and often blamed themselves. Quotes: "My voice just doesn't register clearly with it" (P29); "everybody doesn't use the same language, or have the same vocabulary" (P26). Authors recommend an assets-based rather than deficit view of users and moving beyond demanding that users code-switch. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9307214/))
- Michel, Kaur, Gillespie, Gleason, Wilson and Ghosh, "'It's not a representation of me': Examining Accent Bias and Digital Exclusion in Synthetic AI Voice Services", FAccT 2025 (preprint arXiv 2504.09346; the FAccT DOI 10.1145/3715275.3732018 was reported in a search summary and not checked on the ACM page). Design: two surveys (250 analysed participants each, Prolific) rating stock and cloned voices, plus 26 semi-structured interviews with native English speakers of five accents. In interviews, each participant recorded a one-minute passage, the team built clones with Speechify and ElevenLabs, and participants heard their original then the labelled clones and rated accent mimicry and how closely each clone recreated their voice. (Source: [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1))
- Michel et al.: interviewees said Speechify clones often neutralized or misrepresented their accents; participants tied accent to identity, culture and class; one called a default Indian voice option "like mockery", another said AI voices were built "with some other people in mind". Average accent mimicry accuracy was 69% (Speechify) and 78.5% (ElevenLabs). The authors argue such tools can reinforce linguistic privilege and accent-based discrimination. (Source: [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1))
- Markl (FAccT 2022), "Language Variation and Algorithmic Bias: Understanding Algorithmic Bias in British English Automatic Speech Recognition", pp. 521 to 534, DOI 10.1145/3531146.3533117. Markl's related thesis argues commercial ASR for British Englishes reproduces and can entrench linguistic discrimination. (Citation and thesis summary only; Source: [Edinburgh Research Archive](https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/41277))
- Finnish context: the Lahjoita puhetta (Donate Speech) corpus has about 3,600 hours of everyday spoken Finnish from over 20,000 speakers across Finland; the authors note colloquial Finnish truncates or combines words and uses forms not used in written text, and that models trained on the formal Finnish Parliament corpus do not generalise to colloquial speech. (Search summary of the paper; Source: [Moisio et al., Language Resources and Evaluation, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/); [arXiv 2203.12906](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.12906))

### Inferences
- Inference: harms documented for racialized English dialects (othering, identity threat, self-blame, code-switching) are a plausible risk model for regional or colloquial Finnish speakers whose speech is "corrected" into standard written Finnish by ASR or flattened in English. The size and nature of the effect may differ: the US studies involve race and a history of discrimination, which is not the same as Finnish regional variation. Assumption: regional Finnish dialects carry social meaning (rural/urban, class, region) strong enough that misrepresentation could feel personal; this was not verified in this search.
- Inference: the Lahjoita puhetta finding implies ASR on colloquial or dialectal Finnish is a known weak point, so misrecognition and standardization in the study outputs are likely, not rare.
- Inference: all three designs combine self-report (diary or rating) with interviews, which gives a precedent for pairing a rating task on one's own output with an open interview.

### Gaps
- Could not read Markl 2022 FAccT full text; its specific findings are not reported.
- Did not find studies where speakers saw a translated (not only transcribed or cloned) version of their own dialectal speech.
- No Finnish user-experience study of ASR dialect errors was found.

## Q3. Ethics: risks of showing stereotyping or flattening output, debriefing, withdrawal of data, TENK guidance on when review is needed, GDPR and voice data, consent content

### Takeaway
Under TENK's 2019 guidelines, an end-of-session review of one's own AI output does not by itself trigger mandatory ethical review unless it involves deviation from informed consent, minors under 15 without guardian consent or information, "exceptionally strong stimuli", or risk of mental harm beyond normal daily life. However, informed consent must truthfully describe the procedure and risks, and withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent. Voice recordings are personal data; the EDPB calls voice data "inherently biometric personal data", and sending recordings to an external AI provider makes that provider a recipient (and possibly a third-country transfer) that must be disclosed.

### Cited Findings
TENK (Finnish National Board on Research Integrity), "The ethical principles of research with human participants and ethical review in the human sciences in Finland", TENK publications 3/2019, 2nd revised edition (Source for all TENK items: [TENK guidelines 2019, English PDF](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf); [TENK guidelines page](https://tenk.fi/en/advice-and-materials/guidelines-ethical-review-human-sciences)):
- Section 4.2 (exact list): the researcher "must request an ethical review statement from a human sciences ethics committee" if the research contains any of: (a) participation deviates from the principle of informed consent; (b) intervening in physical integrity; (c) focus on minors under 15 without separate parental or carer consent or without informing a parent or carer so they could prevent participation; (d) exposure to "exceptionally strong stimuli"; (e) "a risk of causing mental harm that exceeds the limits of normal daily life" to participants or those closest to them; (f) a threat to the safety of participants or researchers or their families. Skipping review when one applies "may constitute a violation of responsible conduct of research". A statement "cannot be requested once the research has commenced".
- Definitions: exceptionally strong stimuli "may be, for example, data containing violence or pornography", and may also include designs where participants "are deliberately presented with ideas and data that are completely incompatible with their values". Mental harm beyond daily life may arise, for example, with traumatic experiences; "Research situations can and may, however, include mental strain and emotional experiences similar to situations of everyday life."
- Deviation from informed consent is acceptable only if, among other things, data collection does not cause harm and "the real content and purpose of the study are explained to participants as soon as this is possible" (the TENK equivalent of debriefing after deception).
- Participant rights: voluntary participation; to discontinue at any time without negative consequences, including withdrawing from "an individual phase of the research"; but "Discontinued participation in the research does not prevent the use of research data that has already been gathered"; to withdraw consent at any time, and "It must be as easy to withdraw consent as it is to give it"; to receive "an understandable and truthful view of the aims of the research and any potential harm and risks"; information on processing of personal data, in a language the participant understands.
- Research consent versus GDPR consent: "Informed consent to participate in research is not the same thing as consent which is used as a legal basis for processing personal data"; if consent is the GDPR legal basis it must meet GDPR requirements; the choice of legal basis is the data controller's responsibility.
- Community harm: to avoid unnecessary harm "to research participants and the communities they represent", researchers should familiarise themselves with the community, its culture and history in advance.
- TENK's privacy notice checklist includes recipients or categories of recipients of personal data, information on transfer of personal data to third countries, storage period, legal basis, and (where consent is the basis) the right to withdraw consent without affecting earlier lawful processing. TENK lists "biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" among special categories.
- Nearly all Finnish universities and research organisations have committed to the guidelines and must arrange ethical review for their researchers. (Source: [TENK news 16.9.2019](https://tenk.fi/en/news/ethical-principles-and-ethical-review-human-sciences-second-edition-now-published))

EDPB, Guidelines 02/2021 on Virtual Voice Assistants, version 2.0 adopted 7 July 2021 (Source: [EDPB PDF v2.0](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)):
- Para 31: voice data "may carry personal data both in its content (meaning of the spoken text) and its meta-information (sex or age of the speaker etc.)". "The EDPB recalls that voice data is inherently biometric personal data." When processed to uniquely identify a person, or when it is or is determined to be special category data, processing needs an Article 6 legal basis plus an Article 9 derogation.
- Para 105: "Anonymizing voice recordings is specially challenging, as it is possible to identify users through the content of the message itself and the characteristics of voice itself."

Precedents for safeguards in comparable studies:
- Michel et al. (FAccT 2025): IRB approval, informed consent, interviews to stop if distress arose, debrief offered; no participant reported distress and none withdrew; identifying information removed and data deleted after analysis; authors flagged that the study could inadvertently promote impersonation scams. (Source: [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1))
- Mengesha et al.: written informed consent and separate data-usage consent, USD 150 compensation; the reviewed text does not describe specific wellbeing safeguards for distress; the authors stress protecting privacy when collecting voice samples. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002))
- Robertson and Díaz ran debrief interviews after the MT-mediated sessions. (Source: [Robertson and Díaz 2022](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf))

### Inferences
- Inference (not a ruling): showing participants a standardized or misrecognized transcript of their own speech is most likely within "mental strain and emotional experiences similar to situations of everyday life" (many Finns already use voice typing and auto-captions), so it is unlikely by itself to meet TENK criteria (d) or (e). The judgment could change if outputs are likely to be mocking or stereotyping (Michel et al.'s "like mockery" report), if participants belong to a minority whose speech is socially stigmatized, or if the synthetic English voice clones the participant's own voice. Because review cannot be requested after data collection starts, the safe course is to ask the local human sciences ethics committee before starting.
- Inference: if the consent form does not mention that AI outputs of their own speech will be shown, the design arguably "deviates from informed consent" (criterion a), which triggers mandatory review. Naming the end-of-session review in the consent text avoids this.
- Inference: consent and privacy information should name (1) that recordings are processed by an external AI provider (name the provider, its role as processor or controller, location and any third-country transfer), (2) that AI transcripts, English translations and a synthetic English voice will be produced and shown, (3) that outputs may misrecognize, standardize or misrepresent how they speak, and (4) what withdrawal means after they see the outputs. TENK permits continued use of already gathered data after discontinuation, but if GDPR consent is the legal basis, withdrawal stops further processing; offering a post-viewing option to withdraw recordings and outputs is a design choice that should be recorded with its reason.
- Inference: because voice is hard to anonymize (EDPB para 105), and dialect itself can point to a region or small community, quoted transcripts or audio of regional speech in publications carry re-identification risk.
- Assumption: the EDPB VVA guidelines address voice assistants, not research; applying para 31 to research recordings is by analogy. Whether the study's processing is "for the purpose of uniquely identifying" a person (Article 9) depends on the provider's processing, for example if voice cloning builds a voiceprint; this needs checking with the university data protection officer.
- Inference: framing the review as evaluating the system (not the participant's speech), stating in advance that errors on dialect are a known system limitation, and debriefing about why errors happen, addresses the self-blame reported by Mengesha et al. (17%) and Harrington et al.

### Gaps
- Did not find TENK text specifically on showing participants AI-generated material about themselves, or on debriefing outside deception designs.
- Did not check the Finnish Data Protection Ombudsman's guidance on scientific research (Data Protection Act 1050/2018, section 4) or the EDPB guidance on processors and international transfers; those would ground the external AI provider point.
- No empirical evidence found on whether seeing AI misrepresentation of one's own dialect in a single lab session causes lasting distress; the comparable studies report either everyday-use distress (diary studies) or no reported distress in lab sessions (Michel et al.).
- Did not verify whether the University of Turku or Tampere ethics committee guidance adds sector-specific criteria (TENK allows committees to make the criteria more concrete).

## Q4. Have participants' evaluations of their own outputs been used as primary data, and how should it be framed?

### Takeaway
Yes, in several forms: participants rating AI clones of their own voice (Michel et al.), rating perceived ASR quality of their own transcripts (Hara and Iqbal), recording and reflecting on failures of ASR on their own speech in diaries (Mengesha et al.; Harrington et al.), and post-editing machine translations of their own messages to establish intended meaning (Robertson and Díaz). Self-evaluation is strongest for intended meaning and identity fit, and weakest for target-language adequacy.

### Cited Findings
- Michel et al.: interviewees heard their own recording, then labelled clones, and rated accent mimicry quality and closeness to their own voice; these ratings plus interview reactions were primary data. (Source: [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1))
- Hara and Iqbal: participants rated agreement with "there were no noticeable errors in transcription" for their own recognized speech; this rating was not collected when no transcript was shown because "participants had no mean to assess the speech recognition quality". (Source: [Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf))
- Robertson and Díaz: when reporting the intended meaning of non-English messages, they "use participants' post-edits, i.e., their own translations of their messages to English". They note as a limitation that bilingual participants can infer meaning that monolingual users could not, and suggest future work hire professional translators to assess translation quality. (Source: [Robertson and Díaz 2022](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf))
- Mengesha et al. recommend research on how African American users "want their speech transcribed", which treats speakers' own preferences about representation of their speech as data. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002))
- Harrington et al. recommend an assets-based view of users rather than treating dialect speakers as deficient. (Source: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9307214/))

### Inferences
- Inference: a defensible framing for the planned study is to treat the speaker as the authority on (1) what they meant, (2) whether the Finnish transcript represents what they said, and (3) whether the output represents them acceptably (identity fit, stereotyping), and to treat their judgment of English translation adequacy as perception data, cross-checked by independent bilingual or professional evaluation (as Robertson and Díaz recommend).
- Inference: following Hara and Iqbal, any rating of "errors" should be anchored to something the participant can see or hear (their transcript, their original recording) rather than to output they cannot verify.
- Inference: wording such as "how well did the system capture what you said and how you said it" places fault on the system, which fits the assets-based framing and may reduce self-blame.

### Gaps
- No study found that used Finnish speakers' self-evaluations of speech translation output, or that compared speakers' self-evaluation of translations with expert evaluation of the same items in a speech-to-speech setting.
- No formal guidance (ISO, ACL, TENK) found on using source speakers' evaluations of their own MT output as primary data; the precedents above are study designs, not standards.
