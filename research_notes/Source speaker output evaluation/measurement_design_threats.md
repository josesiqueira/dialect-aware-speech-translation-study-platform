# Measurement design and validity threats when source speakers rate AI transcripts and translations of their own speech

Scope: Finnish to English AI speech translation study. After the speaking tasks and a first questionnaire, each Finnish-speaking participant sees the Finnish ASR transcript of each of their utterances and two English translations (default prompt vs preservation-aware prompt), possibly with synthetic English audio, and rates them on: semantic fidelity, naturalness, representational adequacy, formality, stance, regional association, voice similarity.

Verification note: every source below was checked on a real page in this session (Crossref API record, ACL Anthology page or PDF, ITU page, ISCA Archive page, OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar abstract, or the publisher PDF). Where only the metadata (title, authors, venue) could be verified and not the content, the entry says "metadata only" and no content claim is made from it. Books and preprints are labelled. Statements under "Inferences" are my reasoning, not source statements.

## 1. Rating methods and scales: which suit judging two translation conditions side by side vs separately, and how reliable are they

### Takeaway
Comparative presentation (two outputs of the same source side by side, or ranking / best-worst choices) gives more reliable judgments than rating each output alone, and WMT-style Direct Assessment uses a 100-point slider with per-rater z-score standardisation and built-in quality control items. For this study, the most defensible design is to show both English versions of an utterance together (positions randomised, conditions unlabelled), collect a separate rating per construct for each version, and add a forced-choice preference, because category scales applied to single outputs have low agreement and MOS-style results are sensitive to scale details and instructions.

### Cited Findings
- ITU-T P.800 "Methods for subjective determination of transmission quality" was approved 1996-08-30 and is listed as in force ([ITU-T P.800 page](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.800-199608-I)).
- P.800 describes three methods: Absolute Category Rating (ACR, one stimulus at a time on a 5-point Bad to Excellent scale, averaged into a MOS), Degradation Category Rating (DCR, rate the degradation of a processed sample against a reference) and Comparison Category Rating (CCR, which compares two samples and can show whether the test system is better or worse than the reference; recommended when differences are small). Secondary sources, not the ITU text itself: ([FORCE Technology product sheet on P.800 methods](https://forcetechnology.com/-/media/force-technology-media/pdf-files/unnumbered/senselab/product-sheet-itu-t-p,-d-,800-methods.pdf); [Wikipedia, Absolute Category Rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_Category_Rating)).
- ITU-R BS.1534-3 "Method for the subjective assessment of intermediate quality level of audio systems" (MUSHRA) was approved 2015-10-14 and is in force ([ITU-R BS.1534-3 page](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1534-3-201510-I/en)).
- MUSHRA presents a labelled reference, several test samples, a hidden copy of the reference and low and mid-range anchors (typically 3.5 kHz and 7 kHz low-pass versions of the reference) on the same screen, to calibrate the scale. Secondary source ([Wikipedia, MUSHRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUSHRA)).
- Direct Assessment (Graham, Baldwin, Moffat, Zobel, Natural Language Engineering 23(1), 3-30; online 2015, issue 2017): translations are rated individually on a 100-point slider with hidden numbers (only the midpoint and quarter points marked), each rater's scores are standardised to z-scores, fluency and adequacy are collected in separate tasks, and rater reliability is checked against the rater's own consistency rather than against experts, using repeated items and deliberately degraded "bad reference" items (words deleted for adequacy, words duplicated for fluency); raters whose repeat-pair differences are not significantly smaller than their bad-reference differences are excluded. Direct estimates found substantially more significant system differences than relative preference judgments in their WMT-12 replication ([Graham et al. 2017, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462); [Crossref record 10.1017/S1351324915000339](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/S1351324915000339)).
- WMT 2007 meta-evaluation: inter-annotator kappa for 5-point absolute fluency was .250 and for adequacy .226; sentence ranking .373; constituent ranking .540. Intra-annotator kappa: fluency .537, adequacy .468, sentence ranking .623. The authors wrote that the fluency and adequacy kappa values "should give us pause", that inter-annotator agreement was "barely considered fair" and intra-annotator agreement "only moderate" ([Callison-Burch et al. 2007, WMT, ACL Anthology W07-0718](https://aclanthology.org/W07-0718/)).
- Belz and Kow (2011) compared a visual analogue (continuous) scale with a 7-point discrete scale across six experiments: both were similarly valid and interchangeable in mean system scores; the continuous scale found more significant system differences in 3 of 4 cases; raters strongly preferred the continuous scale ([Belz and Kow 2011, ACL, P11-2040](https://aclanthology.org/P11-2040/)).
- Best-worst scaling produced significantly more reliable annotations than a rating scale for the same total number of annotations (sentiment intensity task) ([Kiritchenko and Mohammad 2017, ACL, P17-2074](https://aclanthology.org/P17-2074/)).
- RankME (continuous scales plus relative assessment of several outputs at once) significantly improved reliability and consistency of human ratings over traditional methods, and allowed rating multiple distinct criteria ([Novikova, Dušek, Rieser 2018, NAACL, N18-2012](https://aclanthology.org/N18-2012/)).
- Side-by-side MQM and side-by-side relative ranking reached higher inter-annotator agreement than point-wise MQM, marked errors more consistently (about 38.5% for explicitly compared systems) and revealed subtle errors missed in point-wise MQM, without changing absolute system scores; side-by-side ranking alone was cheaper but less able to capture subtle differences ([Song, Riley, Deutsch, Freitag 2025, ACL, 2025.acl-long.1002](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1002/)).
- MQM (explicit error span annotation with category and severity) by professional translators with full document context gave a substantially different system ranking from WMT crowd workers, with a clear preference for human over machine output ([Freitag et al. 2021, TACL 9, 1460-1474](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1162/tacl_a_00437)).
- Error Span Annotation (ESA) combines DA's continuous rating with MQM-style severity span marking; the authors describe MQM as expert-only and time-consuming and DA as simpler but less reliable, and report ESA reaches MQM-level quality faster and cheaper without MQM experts ([Kocmi et al. 2024, WMT, 2024.wmt-1.131](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.131/)).
- Pairwise ranking showed a stronger preference for human over machine translation when raters judged whole documents rather than isolated sentences ([Läubli, Sennrich, Volk 2018, EMNLP, D18-1512](https://aclanthology.org/D18-1512/)).
- In TTS evaluation, most Interspeech and SSW 2021 to 2022 papers did not report scale labels, increments or instructions; it was often unclear whether listeners rated naturalness or overall quality; quality MOS was on average higher than naturalness MOS; and changing only the scale increment or the instruction changed MOS for some systems ([Kirkland et al. 2023, SSW, ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/ssw_2023/kirkland23_ssw.html); [Crossref 10.21437/ssw.2023-7](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.21437/ssw.2023-7)).
- For a MOS naturalness test, a stable level of significance was only reached with more than 30 listeners; over 60% of Interspeech 2014 papers used fewer than 20 ([Wester, Valentini-Botinhao, Henter 2015, Interspeech, OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.21437/interspeech.2015-689)).

### Inferences
- The core contrast (default vs preservation-aware translation of the same utterance) is a paired comparison of two outputs of one source, which is the case where CCR, side-by-side MQM / ranking and best-worst methods have their reliability advantage. A design that rates each version on a separate screen loses that advantage and adds memory load.
- Recommended hybrid: show both versions of one utterance on one screen (left/right position randomised per item, neutral labels such as "Version 1" and "Version 2"), collect one rating per construct per version (so each construct has two scores per item and a within-item difference), then one forced-choice "which better represents how you said it" item. This gives both absolute and comparative data from one screen. Using a 100-point slider with z-scoring per participant follows DA practice; a 5 or 7-point labelled scale is acceptable if analysed with ordinal models (Section 7).
- MUSHRA's hidden reference and anchors do not transfer directly because there is no "perfect" English reference of the participant's speech. A partial analogue is a deliberately degraded translation used as a quality control item, as in Graham et al.'s bad-reference items (see Section 2).
- Because Kirkland et al. show that scale increments and instructions change MOS, the exact wording, labels and increments for each construct should be fixed in the protocol and reported, and constructs should not be merged into "overall quality".
- MQM / ESA error annotation needs bilingual expertise; it suits a separate expert rating of semantic fidelity, not the participant session.

### Gaps
- I did not read the ITU P.800 or BS.1534 recommendation texts themselves (only the ITU listing pages); method descriptions above come from secondary pages. Before citing exact clause content (e.g., P.800 annex letters, number of listeners), download the recommendations from the ITU site.
- No source found that tests side-by-side vs separate presentation specifically for register, stance or identity-related judgments (as opposed to overall quality or errors).

## 2. Blinding, randomisation, counterbalancing, carryover, number of items and fatigue

### Takeaway
Within-subject comparison is statistically efficient but exposed to practice, sensitisation and carryover; these are controlled by blinding conditions, randomising item order and on-screen position per participant, and keeping the session short, because answers late in a questionnaire become faster, shorter and more uniform. Quality control items in the style of DA's repeated and degraded items let the analysis detect inattentive or inconsistent raters.

### Cited Findings
- Within-subjects designs remove subject variance from error terms but are "often faulted" for context effects of practice, sensitisation and carry-over; the article discusses procedures to minimise these when they are not wanted ([Greenwald 1976, Psychological Bulletin 83, 314-320, OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0033-2909.83.2.314)).
- Charness, Gneezy and Kuhn (2012), "Experimental methods: Between-subject and within-subject design", Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization 81, 1-8 (metadata only) ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jebo.2011.08.009)).
- In a web survey with randomly ordered blocks, questions positioned later were answered faster, with shorter open answers and less variability in grid items; a longer stated length reduced the number of people who started and completed ([Galesic and Bosnjak 2009, Public Opinion Quarterly 73, 349-360, OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/poq/nfp031)).
- DA quality control: repeated items and degraded "bad reference" items embedded in each task, with rater exclusion when repeats are not more consistent than degraded items ([Graham et al. 2017, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462)).
- In Martindale and Carpuat's survey, every participant saw the same three good translations in the same fixed order before the manipulated item, and the same good translation afterwards "to see if changes in trust would carry over to the next translation" (i.e., carryover was treated as an outcome, not a nuisance) ([Martindale and Carpuat 2018, AMTA, W18-1803 PDF](https://aclanthology.org/W18-1803.pdf)).
- A properly powered repeated-measures reaction time experiment needs at least 1,600 observations per condition (e.g., 40 participants by 40 stimuli); effect sizes reported depend on the number of stimuli ([Brysbaert and Stevens 2018, Journal of Cognition 1, OpenAlex/Semantic Scholar abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.5334/joc.10)).
- Power matters especially when testing for ties between systems: a non-significant difference from a low-powered test can be a Type II error; the paper gives indications of minimum sample sizes for MT human evaluation ([Graham, Haddow, Koehn 2020, EMNLP, 2020.emnlp-main.6](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.6/)).

### Inferences
- Blinding: conditions must never be labelled by name ("default", "preservation-aware", "dialect-aware") on screen, in file names visible to participants, or in the experimenter's speech. Left/right position should be randomised per item (not just per participant) and logged, so position can be a covariate.
- Item order: randomise the order of utterances per participant, or at least randomise task blocks, so that later-position fatigue (Galesic and Bosnjak) is spread across tasks. Log presentation order for use as a covariate.
- Carryover specific to this study: after a few items, a participant can learn that one version is consistently more informal or dialect-like. From then on, ratings may reflect a learned identity of "the informal one" rather than the item. Per-item position randomisation slows this learning but cannot stop it; including presentation trial number by condition in the model lets the analysis test whether the condition gap grows over trials (a sign of learned contrast).
- Number of items: Brysbaert and Stevens' 1,600 observations per condition is derived from reaction-time priming studies, not ratings; it signals that a small number of participants times a small number of items is likely underpowered, but the actual target should come from a simulation-based power analysis on pilot rating data (Assumption: ratings variance unknown until the pilot).
- Fatigue: seven constructs times two versions times N utterances multiplies quickly (e.g., 20 utterances gives 280 ratings plus audio). Options: rate only a subset of constructs per utterance using a planned missing design, or cap utterances per participant and offer a break; record session duration per screen.
- Quality control analogue: embed one or two items where one version is a deliberately wrong translation (meaning changed) to check that participants with enough English detect meaning errors. This doubles as a check of English proficiency for fidelity ratings (Section 5). Debrief participants about these items.

### Gaps
- No source found giving a validated maximum number of translation-rating items per session before fatigue degrades quality; the Galesic and Bosnjak evidence is from general web surveys.
- Greenwald's and Charness et al.'s specific recommended counterbalancing procedures were not read in full (abstract or metadata only).

## 3. Demand characteristics, experimenter expectancy, social desirability, hypothesis guessing, neutral instructions

### Takeaway
Participants who learn or guess a study's hypothesis tend to confirm it, and post-session suspicion probes may not detect this awareness; on the other hand, large online survey experiments found that telling participants the hypothesis did not change treatment effects. The risk is higher here than in an anonymous online survey because the study is in person, the stimuli are the participant's own speech, and the contrast between versions can make the hypothesis (AI flattens informality or dialect) obvious, so instructions, labels and the experimenter's behaviour must not signal it.

### Cited Findings
- Orne (1962), "On the social psychology of the psychological experiment: With particular reference to demand characteristics and their implications", American Psychologist 17, 776-783 (metadata only; the source of the term "demand characteristics") ([Crossref 10.1037/h0043424](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/h0043424)).
- When a confederate told participants (N = 100) the study's purported hypothesis, participants tended to respond in ways that confirmed it; this depended on attitudes toward the experiment or experimenter and individual differences; suspicion probes "may be ineffective" in measuring participants' prior knowledge and suspicion ([Nichols and Maner 2008, Journal of General Psychology 135, 151-166, abstract via Semantic Scholar](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3200/GENP.135.2.151-166)).
- Across over 12,000 participants and five replicated online survey experiments, randomly giving participants information about experimenter intent did not alter treatment effects, and even financial incentives to respond in line with expectations failed to consistently produce demand effects; the authors argue online survey experiments have features that make them robust to demand effects ([Mummolo and Peterson 2019, American Political Science Review 113, 517-529](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/S0003055418000837)).
- Rosenthal and Rubin (1978), "Interpersonal expectancy effects: the first 345 studies", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 1, 377-386 (metadata only; a synthesis of experimenter expectancy studies) ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/S0140525X00075506)).
- Krumpal (2013, online 2011), "Determinants of social desirability bias in sensitive surveys: a literature review", Quality and Quantity 47, 2025-2047 (metadata only) ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/s11135-011-9640-9)).
- Tourangeau, Rips and Rasinski (2000), The Psychology of Survey Response, Cambridge University Press (book): the book covers comprehension, recall, estimation, getting responses into the required format, and "distortions introduced into surveys by deliberate misreporting", and shows how "seemingly unimportant features of the survey can affect the answers obtained" ([Cambridge book record, OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1017/CBO9780511819322)).

### Inferences
- The demand risk in this study has three parts: (a) the hypothesis is easy to infer from the stimuli once a participant sees one version keep their slang or dialect and the other not; (b) the setting is in person with a researcher, unlike the online settings in Mummolo and Peterson, so their reassuring result does not transfer directly; (c) the material is the participant's own voice and words, which may raise self-presentation concerns (e.g., not wanting to endorse a translation that sounds "uneducated", or wanting to defend their dialect). Mummolo and Peterson is the best counter-evidence and should be cited when arguing that the residual demand risk is bounded.
- Neutral instruction wording (draft, for piloting): "You will see your own words written in Finnish, and two English versions produced by computer systems. The versions may differ in many ways, or very little. There are no right or wrong answers. For each version, tell us how you see it." Avoid words that name the hypothesis or the manipulated property: "dialect", "flatten", "standardise", "preserve", "identity", "bias", "default", "improved". Present every construct question with balanced wording (e.g., formality as a bipolar scale "very informal ... very formal" rather than "Is it too formal?").
- Construct questions that name identity ("Does this represent who I am?") are themselves a cue. Placing these after the more neutral constructs (fidelity, naturalness) within each item, and keeping the order fixed across items, limits the cue to one place; alternatively ask representational adequacy once per utterance after both versions are rated on the other constructs. This is a trade-off with question-order effects (Section 4) and should be decided in piloting.
- Experimenter expectancy: run the rating phase as self-paced software with scripted instructions; the experimenter should not know which side holds which condition (the software randomises), should not look at the screen during rating, and should answer questions from a script.
- Hypothesis awareness: include a post-session open question ("What do you think this study was about?") and code it, but per Nichols and Maner treat it as an imperfect measure; analyse whether the condition effect differs between participants who did and did not state the hypothesis (exploratory).
- Ethics vs blinding: the consent form must describe the study truthfully. A truthful but general description ("how AI systems transcribe and translate spoken Finnish, and how speakers perceive the output") avoids naming the hypothesis; full disclosure of the specific hypothesis can be given in the debrief. Assumption: the ethics board accepts this level of disclosure; confirm locally.

### Gaps
- Orne's, Rosenthal and Rubin's and Krumpal's content was not read in this session (metadata only); cite them for the concept names, and read them before quoting any specific claim (e.g., Orne's "good subject" role or post-experimental inquiry).
- No source found that measures demand effects specifically in translation or speech technology user studies.

## 4. Question order effects and priming: why the speaking-experience questionnaire must come first, and anchoring on the first translation seen

### Takeaway
Answering one question makes related information more accessible and shifts later judgments toward it (assimilation), unless the questions are framed as belonging to one conversational context, which can reverse the effect. Showing the AI output first would make translation quality, register and dialect salient and would reveal the hypothesis, so any self-report about the speaking experience, self-perceived speech style and identity must be collected before the reveal; within the rating phase, the first version seen on each screen should be randomised.

### Cited Findings
- Answering a specific question increases the accessibility of information relevant to it; when a specific and a general question are merely placed in sequence, the answer to the general question becomes similar to the specific one (assimilation); when the two are presented as belonging to one conversational context, respondents disregard the primed information to avoid redundancy, following Grice's conversational norms ([Strack, Martin, Schwarz 1988, European Journal of Social Psychology 18, 429-442, OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1002/ejsp.2420180505)).
- Tourangeau, Rips and Rasinski (2000) (book) review how seemingly unimportant survey features affect answers, including comprehension, recall and estimation processes and "the sources of the apparent instability of public opinion" ([OpenAlex book record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1017/CBO9780511819322)).
- Schuman and Presser, Questions and Answers in Attitude Surveys: Experiments on Question Form, Wording, and Context, Academic Press, 1981 (book; existence verified through journal reviews in Social Forces 1983 and Journal of Marketing Research 1982) ([Crossref review record, Social Forces](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/sf/62.2.546); [Crossref review record, JMR](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2307/3151742)).
- Tversky and Kahneman (1974), "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases", Science 185, 1124-1131 (metadata only; the standard citation for anchoring and adjustment) ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)).
- Martindale and Carpuat asked participants about familiarity with and opinion of machine translation before showing any output "to establish a baseline trust level and to account for biases" ([Martindale and Carpuat 2018, W18-1803 PDF](https://aclanthology.org/W18-1803.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Order of the session (recommended): speaking tasks, then questionnaire 1 (speaking experience, self-rated formality / dialect use of one's own speech, language attitudes, English proficiency self-report), then an objective English test (Section 5), then the transcript and translation rating phase, then a short post-questionnaire (hypothesis awareness, overall attitudes), then debrief. Reason: questionnaire 1 measures the participant's own view of their speech before any AI version exists in their mind; if it came after, their self-description would be assimilated toward (or contrasted against) the translations they had just read (Strack et al.), and the self-rating could no longer serve as an independent baseline for "representational adequacy".
- Attitude items about AI (trust, expectations of AI translation) belong before the reveal as well, for the same reason as Martindale and Carpuat's baseline.
- Anchoring within each screen: the version read first may set the reference for the second. Randomising left/right per item, and logging which was read first, lets the model estimate a position effect; if eye or click order is not logged, left position is a proxy (Assumption: Finnish readers read left to right first).
- Anchoring across items: the first few utterances set each participant's personal scale. Z-scoring per participant (DA practice) or participant random intercepts and thresholds (Section 7) absorb level differences, and a few practice items with no analysis value at the start reduce early-scale drift.
- Construct order inside each screen: asking "Does this sound like me?" before "Is the meaning right?" may make identity salient and colour the fidelity judgment. Keep construct order fixed and the same for both versions; consider randomising construct order between participants (not items) and including it as a factor, if the sample allows.

### Gaps
- Schuman and Presser's and Tourangeau et al.'s specific chapters on context effects were not read in this session; cite them as books for the general phenomenon only, or read the relevant chapters before quoting.
- Anchoring evidence specific to sequential translation quality judgments was not found.

## 5. Who can judge what: proficiency, fluency over adequacy, bilingual competence, and whether source speakers are the right judges

### Takeaway
Raters who cannot verify meaning respond to fluency more than to adequacy, and professional or expert raters agree more and discriminate better than non-experts, so semantic fidelity ratings from participants with limited English are not valid fidelity measures on their own. Source speakers are the only possible judges of representational adequacy (whether a version represents how they speak and who they are), but semantic fidelity should also be judged by independent bilingual experts, and every participant's English proficiency should be measured objectively, because self-ratings predict proficiency worse than a short vocabulary test.

### Cited Findings
- In a survey of non-specialist MT users (US Qualtrics panel; participants first saw only the MT output, without the source), users "responded strongly to disfluent translations, but were, surprisingly, much less concerned with adequacy" ([Martindale and Carpuat 2018, AMTA, W18-1803](https://aclanthology.org/W18-1803/)). Note on scope: this measured trust, not quality ratings, and participants did not see the source text; it supports "fluency dominates when meaning cannot be checked", not a direct claim about bilingual raters.
- Comparing professional translators with non-experts, the experts' judgments showed higher inter-annotator agreement and better discrimination between human and machine translation ([Toral, Castilho, Hu, Way 2018, WMT, W18-6312](https://aclanthology.org/W18-6312/)).
- Perceived quality in human evaluation "depends on the choice of raters, the availability of linguistic context, and the creation of reference translations" ([Läubli et al. 2020, JAIR 67](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1613/jair.1.11371)).
- Expert MQM ratings gave a substantially different system ranking from crowd ratings ([Freitag et al. 2021, TACL](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1162/tacl_a_00437)).
- Untrained evaluators distinguished GPT-3 from human-written text at chance level, gave often contradictory reasons, and short training raised accuracy only to about 55% ([Clark et al. 2021, ACL, 2021.acl-long.565](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.565/)).
- LexTALE, a 5-minute English vocabulary test, predicted English vocabulary knowledge, correlated substantially with general English proficiency, and was generally superior to self-ratings of proficiency in Dutch and Korean L2 English speakers ([Lemhöfer and Broersma 2012, Behavior Research Methods 44, 325-343](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3758/s13428-011-0146-0)).
- In Graham et al.'s DA, both fluency and adequacy were monolingual tasks for target-language speakers, with adequacy judged against a reference translation rather than the source ([Graham et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462)).

### Inferences
- Construct by judge (proposed allocation):
  - Representational adequacy ("represents how I speak / who I am"): source speaker only; no one else has access to the speaker's intended self-presentation. This is the construct that justifies the in-session design.
  - Semantic fidelity: source speaker (knows what they meant, which is an advantage over any external judge) but only valid when the speaker's English is good enough to read the meaning of the English text; also rated by independent Finnish-English bilingual experts (MQM or ESA style) blind to condition, as the reference measure.
  - Naturalness of the English: best judged by fluent English readers; participants' naturalness ratings at low proficiency are likely to be noisy and may track simplicity rather than idiomaticity.
  - Formality and stance: participants can judge whether the English sounds as formal / as certain as they meant, but perceiving English register markers requires proficiency; compare with expert ratings.
  - Regional association: participants cannot easily judge which English regional variety a translation evokes unless they know English varieties; rephrase for participants as "does this sound like it comes from a particular place or group?" and use expert or English-native raters for the English-side regional judgment.
  - Voice similarity: source speaker is a special judge of their own voice (Section 6 notes Rosi et al.), so also collect similarity ratings from listeners familiar and unfamiliar with the speaker, if feasible.
- Proficiency measurement: use an objective test (LexTALE, 5 minutes) plus a self-report (e.g., CEFR self-assessment), and include proficiency as a moderator of the condition effect on each construct. Predicted pattern if Martindale and Carpuat's mechanism applies: at lower proficiency, fidelity ratings correlate more strongly with naturalness ratings.
- An embedded meaning-error item (Section 2) gives a direct, per-participant check: participants who do not penalise a translation with a changed meaning should have their fidelity ratings flagged or down-weighted in sensitivity analyses.
- A back-translation into Finnish to help low-proficiency participants judge fidelity would introduce a third MT system whose errors and register choices contaminate the judgment; avoid it, or treat it as a separate, labelled condition.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly tests whether bilingual source-text authors are better or worse judges of translation adequacy than independent bilingual experts.
- No study found on proficiency-dependent rating behaviour specifically for Finnish L1 readers of English MT.
- "Fluency over adequacy" evidence is from trust measures in monolingual users; evidence for the same bias in rating scales by partially proficient bilinguals was not found.

## 6. Reading one's own ASR transcript and hearing synthetic audio of one's own voice

### Takeaway
I found no study that tests speakers reviewing ASR transcripts of their own speech, so this part rests mainly on inference: ASR errors flow into the translation, and a speaker who sees a wrong Finnish transcript may blame the translation or rate both versions lower, so transcript errors must be recorded as a covariate. For own-voice audio, the one direct study found shows that people rate clones of their own voice differently from unfamiliar listeners, and that perceived similarity to the original speaker predicts more positive trait ratings.

### Cited Findings
- WER and CER do not necessarily capture the usefulness of recognition results; Itoh et al. built a metric replicating human-perceived quality of ASR output (Japanese) from error-type features ([Itoh et al. 2015, Interspeech, ISCA PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/itoh15_interspeech.pdf)).
- In eyes-free review of speech recognition output (listeners hear the recognised text read back), selectively slowing audio where the recogniser was uncertain increased error detection by 12% relative to uniform slowing and cut decision time by 11% (preprint, listed as to appear at PETRA 2025; whether participants reviewed their own speech is not stated on the abstract page) ([Nowrin and Vertanen, arXiv 2410.20564](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20564)).
- Forty-seven participants (and 47 unfamiliar controls) rated trustworthiness, attractiveness, competence and dominance of cloned and recorded samples of their own and a friend's voice. Familiar listeners found clones less (or similarly) trustworthy, attractive and competent than recordings, while unfamiliar listeners rated clones higher; familiar listeners tended to prefer their friend's voice to their own; perceived similarity of self- and friend-voice clones to the original identity predicted higher ratings on all trait scales. The authors suggest unfamiliar listeners' preference may relate to voice synthesis models' tendency "to homogenise speaking accents and styles" toward preferred norms (stated as a possible explanation, not tested) ([Rosi, Soopramanien, McGettigan 2025, Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans 4, 100143, UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10206954/1/1-s2.0-S2949882125000271-main.pdf); [Crossref 10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100143](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100143)).
- People valued their own amateur creations similarly to experts' creations and expected others to share their view (IKEA effect), but only when the task was completed successfully ([Norton, Mochon, Ariely 2012, Journal of Consumer Psychology 22, 453-460](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002)).

### Inferences
- Show the Finnish ASR transcript first and ask the speaker to (a) mark whether it is correct, and (b) optionally correct it or mark the wrong words. Use this as: a measure of ASR error from the speaker's perspective; a covariate in translation analyses (translations of misrecognised utterances are expected to get lower fidelity ratings in both conditions); and a filter for a sensitivity analysis restricted to correctly transcribed utterances. Also compute WER against a researcher-verified reference transcript, since speaker detection is itself imperfect.
- Because both translation conditions receive the same ASR transcript, ASR errors are held constant across conditions within an item, so they add noise but should not bias the condition contrast. They can interact with condition, though: a preservation-aware prompt may handle misrecognised dialect words differently. Include an ASR-error by condition interaction.
- Seeing one's own speech in writing (with disfluencies, dialect spellings or standardised spellings) may itself trigger self-presentation reactions (e.g., embarrassment at fillers). Whether the Finnish transcript is verbatim or cleaned changes what participants compare the translations to; fix and report this choice.
- The IKEA effect is an analogy only (it concerns self-made objects, not one's own speech); it suggests that speakers may defend their own wording and rate versions that keep it more favourably, which would favour the preservation-aware condition independent of quality. Treat as a hypothesis to examine, not an established effect.
- Own-voice audio: Rosi et al. show that self-familiarity changes evaluations of clones, so voice similarity ratings by the speaker are not interchangeable with ratings by unfamiliar listeners; report them separately. Present audio after the text-based ratings, or in a separate block, so that the voice does not colour the text constructs (audio quality could spill over into fidelity and representational adequacy ratings). If synthetic English audio of both versions uses the same voice, voice similarity is not a condition contrast and could be rated once per participant rather than per item, saving time.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on source speakers detecting ASR errors in transcripts of their own speech, or on how transcript errors change speakers' perception of downstream translation quality. This is a genuine gap; the study could report it as a contribution.
- No source verified in this session on the general phenomenon that people find their own recorded voice unfamiliar (bone conduction vs air conduction); look for it separately if it will be cited.

## 7. Statistical analysis: mixed-effects and cumulative link mixed models with participant and item random effects

### Takeaway
Ratings on labelled ordinal scales should be analysed with ordinal (cumulative link) models rather than treating them as metric, since metric models can produce false alarms, missed effects and even inverted effects; random effects should be "maximal" for the design, including by-participant and by-item random slopes for condition. In this study, utterances are unique to each participant, so they are nested within participants rather than crossed; the crossed factor is the shared task or prompt.

### Cited Findings
- Baayen, Davidson and Bates (2008), "Mixed-effects modeling with crossed random effects for subjects and items", Journal of Memory and Language 59, 390-412 (metadata only) ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jml.2007.12.005)).
- Linear mixed-effects models for confirmatory testing generalise best with the maximal random-effects structure justified by the design; random-intercepts-only models on within-subject or within-item data, where subjects or items vary in sensitivity to the manipulation, generalise worse than separate F1 and F2 tests ([Barr, Levy, Scheepers, Tily 2013, Journal of Memory and Language 68, 255-278](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jml.2012.11.001)).
- All surveyed JPSP, Psychological Science and JEP:General articles that analysed Likert-type ordinal data used metric models; treating ordinal data as metric can produce false alarms, loss of power and inversions of effects, also for interactions, and averaging several ordinal items does not fix this; the authors advocate ordered-probit or similar models ([Liddell and Kruschke 2018, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 79, 328-348](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.08.009)).
- Tutorial on cumulative, sequential and adjacent-category ordinal models fitted in a Bayesian framework with the R package brms; ordinal models give better interpretation and inference than metric models for ordinal data ([Bürkner and Vuorre 2019, Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science 2, 77-101](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/2515245918823199)).
- DA standardises each rater's 100-point scores into z-scores before aggregation ([Graham et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462)).
- Power in designs with participants and stimuli depends on both counts; effect sizes depend on the number of stimuli ([Brysbaert and Stevens 2018](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.5334/joc.10)).

### Inferences
- Structure of the data: each rating is (participant, utterance, version/condition, construct). Utterances come from the participant's own speech, so utterance is nested within participant. If all participants performed the same tasks or prompts (e.g., the same picture description or role-play), the task/prompt is crossed with participants and should be a crossed random effect.
- Model per construct (ordinal scale), in lme4-style notation: rating ~ condition * proficiency + position + trial + asr_error * condition + (1 + condition | participant) + (1 + condition | prompt) + (1 | participant:utterance). This is a cumulative link mixed model (e.g., R package ordinal, function clmm, or brms with family cumulative). Assumption: the ordinal package and clmm function exist as I describe; I did not verify the package documentation in this session.
- If the 100-point slider is used, a linear mixed model on raw or per-participant z-scores is defensible (DA practice), but check for floor and ceiling clustering; a beta or ordinal model on binned scores is a sensitivity analysis.
- The forced-choice preference item can be analysed with a mixed logistic model (probability of choosing the preservation-aware version), with position (left/right) as a covariate to estimate side bias.
- Multiple constructs: the seven constructs are correlated; either fit a multivariate model or fit one model per construct with a pre-registered correction for multiple testing. Pre-register which construct is primary (representational adequacy is the natural primary outcome for the source-speaker session).
- If maximal models fail to converge, Barr et al. recommend keeping the maximal structure justified by the design as the goal; a Bayesian fit (brms) with weakly informative priors usually converges where frequentist clmm fails. Report the structure actually fitted.
- Power: simulate from pilot data (participants times utterances per participant times two conditions), following Brysbaert and Stevens' logic; the participant count matters more than utterances per participant for the condition by participant slope.
