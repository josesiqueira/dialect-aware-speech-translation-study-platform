# Public demo booth for a Finnish to English speech translation study: what counts as research data, and how to engage honestly

Context (from the assignment, not a source): booth on 23 October 2026 in Finland. Visitors speak Finnish into the app in Demo Mode and see the ASR transcript, two English translations (default prompt vs preservation-aware prompt) and hear synthetic English speech. The study itself has no results yet.

Source labels used below: [official guidance], [law], [peer-reviewed], [conference paper, peer-reviewed], [preprint], [grey literature], [secondary commentary]. Every source below was opened and read in this session unless marked otherwise in Gaps.

## Q1. Under GDPR and Finnish research ethics, what is needed to use booth visitors' speech or answers as research data? What can be collected anonymously as event feedback? Is voice ever anonymous?

### Takeaway
Using visitors' speech or identifiable answers as research data needs the full research setup in advance: informed consent to participate (TENK), a separate GDPR legal basis chosen by the data controller (in Finnish universities usually "scientific research in the public interest", sometimes consent or legitimate interest), a privacy notice given at collection, data minimisation and documented decisions. Voice recordings should be treated as personal data and are hard to anonymise (EDPB), so "anonymous voice data" is not a safe claim. Truly anonymous, non-recorded engagement (a show-of-hands vote, a tally with no free text, no audio stored) falls outside GDPR (Recital 26), but that does not make it research data, and the booth should not present it as such.

### Cited Findings

TENK guideline (the Finnish humanities/social/behavioural sciences guideline is TENK publication 3/2019, "The ethical principles of research with human participants and ethical review in the human sciences in Finland", 2nd revised edition) [official guidance]:
- Informed consent to participate is "a central ethical principle in research with human participants". The principles apply where the participant interacts with the researcher, "for example as an interviewee", "as a provider of information or as a subject of observation": [TENK 2019, section 3.2](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- TENK states explicitly that informed consent to participate "is not the same thing as consent which is used as a legal basis for processing personal data"; if consent is used as the GDPR basis it must meet GDPR consent requirements, and choosing the legal basis "is the responsibility of the data controller": [TENK 2019, footnote 33](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- Participants' rights listed by TENK: voluntary participation (with particular care where the participant is a student, employee, customer or in another dependent relationship with the research organisation); the researcher documents consent "either orally, in writing, electronically or by other means"; the right to stop at any time; withdrawing consent "must be as easy" as giving it; the right to information about the research and the processing of personal data in a language the participant understands, and "sufficient time to consider"; an "understandable and truthful view of the aims of the research"; and the right "to be aware that they are participating in research": [TENK 2019, section 3.2 a to f](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- Minors: if 15 or older, their own consent is sufficient (parent informed where possible); under 15, participation "is primarily decided by the parent or carer". Informing the parent is sufficient for research "that does not involve the processing of the personal data of the minor participant (for example observation without recording devices and processing of personal data)": [TENK 2019, section 3.3](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- Personal data processing in research must be "planned, responsible and in accordance with the law"; there must be a legal basis (special categories need a separate basis, GDPR Articles 6 and 9); the purpose must be determined "in sufficient detail"; only data "necessary for the purpose of the research" may be collected; decisions "must be justified and clearly documented": [TENK 2019, section 3.5](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- TENK definition: research data contains personal data "if it can be directly or indirectly be used to identify a person", taking into account means "reasonably likely to be used"; special categories include "biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person": [TENK 2019, Terms and definitions](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- Participants must be informed of their rights and the processing "no later than when their personal data is collected", in "truthful and comprehensible language", in a manner "practical and natural in view of the research"; they "cannot be required to obtain information ... themselves": [TENK 2019, section 3.5(e)](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- Participants "must not be promised complete anonymity if this cannot be guaranteed": [TENK 2019, section 3.6(c)](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- If data will be opened, participants "must be informed at the data collection stage": [TENK 2019, section 3.7](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- Ethical review statement is mandatory only for listed designs (deviation from informed consent, physical intervention, minors under 15 without parent consent or informing, exceptionally strong stimuli, risk of mental harm beyond normal daily life, safety threats). Review "is carried out before data is collected. An ethical review statement cannot be issued afterwards.": [TENK 2019, sections 4.1 and 4.2](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)
- The research plan "should address ethical risks ... irrespective of whether or not the research undergoes ethical review": [TENK 2019, section 4.1](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)

GDPR and EU guidance:
- Recital 26: data protection applies to information about an identified or identifiable person; pseudonymised data that can be re-linked is still personal data; identifiability is judged by "all the means reasonably likely to be used", including singling out, by the controller or anyone else, considering cost, time and technology; data protection does not apply to anonymous information, "including for statistical or research purposes" [law]: [GDPR Recital 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-26/)
- EDPB: voice data "may carry personal data both in its content (meaning of the spoken text) and its meta-information (sex or age of the speaker etc.)", and "The EDPB recalls that voice data is inherently biometric personal data." When processed to uniquely identify a person, an Article 9 derogation is needed in addition to an Article 6 basis [official guidance]: [EDPB Guidelines 02/2021 on virtual voice assistants, v2.0, para. 31 and section 3.8](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)
- EDPB: "Anonymizing voice recordings is specially challenging, as it is possible to identify users through the content of the message itself and the characteristics of voice itself", while noting research on voice anonymisation techniques: [EDPB Guidelines 02/2021, para. 105](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)
- EDPB: because of "the inherent difficulties of anonymising voice data", erasure "could be hardly accommodated by anonymising personal datasets", though not ruled out as technology evolves: [EDPB Guidelines 02/2021, para. 160](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)

Finnish practice on legal basis:
- University of Helsinki (statement to the EDPB consultation, 23 June 2026): in Finland "public interest" as a basis "is used widely, as it is defined in the § 4.1. of the Finnish Data Protection Act"; UH considers consent "a problematic basis for data processing in the context of research", partly because universities act as public authorities and consent is incompatible "where the data subject is in a weaker position"; obtaining "consent to participate" while using another legal basis such as public interest "is common practice in Finland" [grey literature, institutional position statement]: [University of Helsinki statement on EDPB Guidelines 1/2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/consultation_feedback/1417/feedback_1417_statement_uh_edpb_data-protection-in-research.pdf)
- The same UH statement says a bachelor's thesis "has not been regarded as meeting the threshold of scientific research from the perspective of the legal basis for processing" at UH, and argues that the university and non-employed researchers (grant-funded researchers, doctoral candidates, students) should be regarded as joint controllers: [University of Helsinki statement](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/consultation_feedback/1417/feedback_1417_statement_uh_edpb_data-protection-in-research.pdf)
- EDPB Guidelines 1/2026 on processing personal data for scientific research were published as a draft for consultation on 16 April 2026 (consultation closed 25 June 2026; not final as of the commentary). They propose an indicative six-factor test for "scientific research": methodical and systematic approach; adherence to ethical standards; verifiability and transparency; autonomy and independence; aim of contributing to general knowledge and wellbeing; potential to contribute to scientific knowledge. They state data should first be anonymised, or at least pseudonymised where research purposes allow [secondary commentary, law firm]: [Hogan Lovells, EDPB Guidelines on processing personal data for scientific research: key takeaways](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/edpb-guidelines-on-processing-personal-data-for-scientific-research-key-takeaways)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Under TENK, anything the booth wants to analyse later as evidence about the study (visitor recordings, transcripts, translation preferences linked to an utterance, free-text comments) makes the visitor a research participant. That needs, before 23 October: a defined purpose in the research plan, a named data controller (likely the university, possibly jointly with the researcher), a chosen GDPR basis (most likely Finnish Data Protection Act section 4(1) public interest, per Finnish practice; to be confirmed with the university data protection officer), a privacy notice available at the booth, a short consent-to-participate step, and a withdrawal route. Doing this properly in two weeks at a busy booth is hard; the honest default is that the booth collects no research data.
- (Inference) Booth audio should be treated as personal data even if no name is collected, because of the EDPB position that voice is inherently biometric personal data and hard to anonymise, and because what visitors say can itself identify them (Recital 26 "singling out"; EDPB para. 105). Demo Mode should therefore not store audio, transcripts or translations, or should discard them at the end of each turn, and the booth sign should say so. If audio is sent to cloud ASR/LLM/TTS APIs, that transfer is itself processing of personal data even in Demo Mode; the sign and privacy information should mention it (assumption: the app uses third-party APIs; check the actual architecture).
- (Inference) Anonymous engagement that never touches personal data (tokens dropped in a jar, a tap counter on a screen with no audio or free text kept, a show of hands) is outside GDPR by Recital 26. It can be reported as "booth engagement" or event feedback, but it is not research data in the TENK sense because visitors were not informed they were taking part in research and the sample is self-selected and uncontrolled. Using it in a paper as evidence would undercut the "understandable and truthful view of the aims" principle.
- (Inference) An event-quality survey (did you enjoy the booth, did you understand the explanation) is closer to service evaluation than to research. The EDPB draft six-factor test (methodical, verifiable, aimed at generalisable knowledge) suggests such a survey usually would not qualify as scientific research processing; that also means it cannot borrow the research legal basis. Keep it anonymous and minimal.
- (Inference) If the researcher wants a real research step at the booth, the cleanest pattern is recruitment, not collection: a QR code or card inviting visitors to sign up later for the actual planned study, with its own information sheet and consent. Signing up collects contact data (personal data), so it still needs a privacy notice, but it keeps demo and study data separate.
- (Inference) Students: if the visitors are students of the researcher's own university, TENK's dependent-relationship caution applies to any research participation.
- (Inference) Minors at a public event: under-15 visitors speaking into the app would be minors' voice data; another reason for Demo Mode to store nothing.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Finnish Data Protection Act (1050/2018) English translation on finlex.fi (404 at the URL tried) or a tietosuoja.fi research page (404), so section 4 is cited only through the University of Helsinki statement, not the statute text.
- I did not read GDPR Articles 6, 9, 13 and 89 directly in this session; TENK and the UH statement refer to them.

## Q2. Precedents: Lahjoita puhetta (Donate Speech) and Mozilla Common Voice

### Takeaway
Both large public speech campaigns treated speech as personal data and built consent and information into the collection app itself before any recording started. Lahjoita puhetta used GDPR legitimate interest with a balancing test and a DPIA, not consent, and did not need TENK ethical review. Common Voice used explicit online donation, optional self-reported demographics, an anonymised speaker ID and a CC0 release with a "do not try to identify speakers" term. Neither precedent recorded people casually at an event without prior information.

### Cited Findings
- Lahjoita puhetta (Moisio et al., Language Resources and Evaluation 57(3):1295-1327, 2023; online 2022; DOI 10.1007/s10579-022-09606-3) [peer-reviewed]: about 3600 hours from over 20,000 speakers (Table 1: 20,890 speakers, 218,146 recordings, 3604.8 h; about 1600 h transcribed): [Moisio et al. 2023, PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)
- Donors "had to acknowledge that they were informed of the conditions of participation before they could start donating"; two documents were drafted, "a short information page including simple conditions of participation, and a more comprehensive data protection policy"; a certain level of language proficiency was needed to understand the privacy notice: [Moisio et al. 2023, Data collection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)
- Legal basis: the speech was collected on the basis of the "legitimate interest" of researchers, universities, research organisations and companies, and "it was necessary to accomplish a balance test"; "Since speech samples may contain personal data, they are protected by ... the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)": [Moisio et al. 2023, Data collection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)
- Ethical review was judged not needed because it applies only to specified research configurations; risks were considered "rather low", but "to be sure, a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) was made": [Moisio et al. 2023, Data collection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)
- "Identities of speakers were not collected explicitly"; one app client was assumed to be one speaker; optional background questions covered dialect, gender, native language, age, residence, birthplace, occupation and education: [Moisio et al. 2023, Metadata](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)
- Engagement: Yle (Finnish Broadcasting Company) helped with marketing, wrote "around 40 straightforward topics, within ten different themes", and broadcast "comical infomercials" on national radio and TV in 2020 to 2021; the campaign won a PRIX EUROPA prize in 2021; the app used one-button start/stop recording: [Moisio et al. 2023, Data collection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)
- Common Voice (Ardila et al., LREC 2020, pp. 4218-4222) [conference paper, peer-reviewed; arXiv preprint version read]: contributors record by reading displayed sentences on the website or iPhone app; other contributors validate clips by up-vote/down-vote; two up-votes make a clip valid; contributors can switch between recording and validating: [Ardila et al. 2020, arXiv:1912.06670](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06670) and [ACL Anthology record](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.520)
- Released files contain "an anonymized ID for the speaker" and demographic data (age, gender, accent) "which was optionally self-reported by the speaker"; all speech data is released under CC0: [Ardila et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06670)
- Dataset card terms: "You agree to not attempt to determine the identity of speakers in the Common Voice dataset"; license "Public Domain, CC-0"; "The dataset consists of people who have donated their voice online" [grey literature, dataset documentation]: [Hugging Face legacy Common Voice card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/legacy-datasets/common_voice)
- Since October 2025 Common Voice datasets are distributed only through Mozilla Data Collective: [Hugging Face Common Voice 17.0 page](https://huggingface.co/datasets/mozilla-foundation/common_voice_17_0)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Both precedents show that public speech collection is feasible but depends on information and acknowledgement before recording, a documented legal basis and (for Lahjoita puhetta) a DPIA. A two-week-away booth is unlikely to have these, which supports "Demo Mode stores nothing".
- (Inference) Lahjoita puhetta's legitimate-interest route plus DPIA shows that a Finnish university consortium did not rely on consent; the booth researcher should not assume consent is the default basis and should ask the university DPO.
- (Inference) Common Voice's "anonymized ID" is pseudonymisation of the identifier, not anonymisation of the voice; the no-identification clause exists because the voice itself remains identifying (consistent with EDPB para. 105). Good booth wording: "we do not keep your voice", not "your voice is anonymous".
- (Inference) The Common Voice validation loop (people judge others' clips) is a precedent for "visitors compare and vote" as an engagement mechanic; at a booth this can be done on prepared examples, without visitor audio.
- (Inference) The booth can point visitors who want to contribute speech to existing, properly consented channels (for example the Language Bank of Finland or Common Voice Finnish) rather than collecting speech itself. I did not verify whether Lahjoita puhetta is still open for donations; treat as an idea only.

### Gaps
- I could not open Mozilla's current Common Voice privacy notice or terms (pages render via JavaScript); claims about withdrawal and deletion from released datasets are unverified.
- Moisio et al. do not describe withdrawal rights, age limits or anonymisation of recordings in the parts read.

## Q3. Science communication evidence on interactive demos and exhibits: what makes visitors engage?

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed evidence supports three mechanics that fit this booth: asking people to commit to a prediction before the reveal (much stronger learning than passive watching), making the experience personally relevant and immediately understandable, and pairing interaction with conversation with the researcher. AI-literacy exhibit research adds: personally meaningful outputs, collaboration and embodiment, and making the AI's steps visible.

### Cited Findings
- Crouch, Fagen, Callan and Mazur (American Journal of Physics 72(6):835-838, 2004; DOI 10.1119/1.1707018) [peer-reviewed]: "students who passively observe demonstrations understand the underlying concepts no better" than students who did not see the demonstration; "Learning is enhanced, however, by increasing student engagement"; "students who predict the demonstration outcome before seeing it, however, display significantly greater understanding": [ComPADRE record with abstract](https://www.compadre.org/IntroPhys/items/detail.cfm?ID=2329)
- Allen (Science Education 88(S1):S17-S33, 2004; DOI 10.1002/sce.20016) [peer-reviewed]: museum exhibits face a "constructivist dilemma"; "To be effective as teaching tools, exhibits need to be highly intrinsically motivating at every step of an interaction"; design should be supported by "a strong program of research and evaluation"; the paper organises Exploratorium findings under immediate apprehendability, physical interactivity, conceptual coherence and diversity of learners: [informalscience.org record](https://informalscience.org/research/designs-learning-studying-science-museum-exhibits-do-more-entertain)
- Long and Magerko, "What is AI Literacy? Competencies and Design Considerations" (CHI 2020; DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376727) [conference paper, peer-reviewed]: public understanding of AI "is often limited"; the paper proposes AI literacy competencies and design considerations for learner-centred AI: [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3313831.3376727?fields=title,venue,year,authors,abstract)
- Long, Blunt and Magerko, "Co-Designing AI Literacy Exhibits for Informal Learning Spaces" (Proc. ACM HCI / CSCW 2021; DOI 10.1145/3476034) [peer-reviewed]: museums are "particularly well-suited" for public communication about AI for audiences wanting a "casual" understanding; five low-fidelity AI exhibit prototypes were tested with family groups, using "collaboration, creativity, and embodiment" as design inspirations: [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3476034?fields=title,venue,year,authors,abstract)
- Follow-up work (IDC 2023, "Fostering AI Literacy with Embodiment and Creativity: From Activity Boxes to Museum Exhibits") focuses on centring learner interests, generating personally meaningful outputs, and embodiment and collaboration at larger scale [conference paper; read via project record and search summary only]: [informalscience.org record](https://informalscience.org/research/fostering-ai-literacy-with-embodiment-creativity-from-activity-boxes-to-museum-exhibits/)
- Living Laboratory model (Museum of Science, Boston) [grey literature, institutional page]: researchers run real studies on the museum floor; visitors can "get answers to their questions about science directly from the source", researchers are "always happy to just talk to you about their research" even with non-participants; anyone joining a study goes through informed consent first; research partners are trained by museum educators to explain the scientific process and their methods: [Museum of Science, Living Laboratory](https://mos.org/living-laboratory)

### Inferences
- (Inference) The single most evidence-backed change for this booth is "predict, then reveal": before the visitor hears the English output, ask them to guess what the default translation will lose (dialect, politeness, a joke, a name, register), then show both translations. Crouch et al. is classroom physics, not a museum or AI study, so the transfer is an assumption, but it matches the "nice, so what?" problem: passive viewing produced no measurable gain.
- (Inference) Personal relevance: letting visitors use their own dialect word, a slang phrase or a family saying makes the output personally meaningful (Long et al.), and this is where the preservation-aware prompt has something to show.
- (Inference) Immediate apprehendability (Allen): the A/B/C chain (Finnish speech to transcript to two translations to synthetic speech) should be visible as one labelled pipeline on screen or a poster, so visitors see where errors enter (ASR vs translation vs TTS).
- (Inference) The Living Laboratory model supports a researcher-present booth where conversation about the method is the main product, and shows that "research happens here" and "demo happens here" can coexist if consented study participation is clearly separate from open play.

### Gaps
- Gutwill's paper on testing visitor consent methods in museums (informalscience archive) could not be fetched (connection refused); the Crowley and Callanan 1998 sticker-consent method appears only in a search snippet and is unverified.
- I did not find a peer-reviewed study specifically on speech translation or language technology demos at public events.
- I did not read Visitor Studies, Curator or JCOM articles in this session; the findings above come from Science Education, American Journal of Physics and ACM venues.

## Q4. Honest communication of a study with no results yet, and of AI limitations and uncertainty

### Takeaway
Experimental evidence shows that openly stating uncertainty causes only a small drop in trust (mostly for vague verbal statements), so the booth can say plainly that the study is planned, has no results, and that the demo is one live example, not evidence. Exaggeration mostly originates with researchers and their institutions, and caveats do not reduce uptake, so the researcher controls overclaiming at the source. AI-specific work warns that researcher-driven capability claims feed public AI hype.

### Cited Findings
- van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman and Spiegelhalter (PNAS 2020; DOI 10.1073/pnas.1913678117) [peer-reviewed]: five experiments including a preregistered replication and a BBC News field experiment (total n = 5,780); "whereas people do perceive greater uncertainty when it is communicated, we observed only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication"; results "could help reassure all communicators ... that they can be more open and transparent about the limits of human knowledge": [Europe PMC record, PMC7149229](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1073/pnas.1913678117&resultType=core&format=json)
- van der Bles et al. (Royal Society Open Science 2019; DOI 10.1098/rsos.181870) [peer-reviewed review]: proposes a framework with three objects of uncertainty (facts, numbers, science) and two levels (direct, indirect); "there is some evidence that communicating epistemic uncertainty does not necessarily affect audiences negatively", but impact "can vary between individuals and communication formats": [Europe PMC record, PMC6549952](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1098/rsos.181870&resultType=core&format=json)
- Sumner et al. (BMJ 2014; DOI 10.1136/bmj.g7015) [peer-reviewed]: of 462 UK university press releases, 40% contained exaggerated advice, 33% exaggerated causal claims, 36% exaggerated inference to humans from animal research; when releases exaggerated, 58%, 81% and 86% of news stories did too, versus 17%, 18% and 10% otherwise; "there was little evidence that exaggeration in press releases increased the uptake of news": [Europe PMC record, PMC4262123](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1136/bmj.g7015&resultType=core&format=json)
- Thais (Proceedings of AIES 2024, 7(1):1455-1465; DOI 10.1609/aies.v7i1.31737) [conference paper, peer-reviewed]: centres "the research community as a key player in the development and proliferation of hype"; overestimated capabilities create "an enhanced risk of subjecting the public to dangerous or harmful" technology and can misdirect research and policy: [AAAI OJS page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31737)
- TENK requires that research participants get "an understandable and truthful view of the aims of the research and any potential harm and risks" and "an accurate account of the effects and potential benefits" [official guidance, applies to participants, used here by analogy for visitors]: [TENK 2019, section 3.2(e)](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Inference) Suggested honest framing for signage and talk: "This is a demo, not a result. We are planning a study that will test whether the preservation-aware prompt keeps more of the speaker in the translation. Today you can see one example at a time; one example cannot tell us which prompt is better." This matches van der Bles et al.: state the uncertainty concretely rather than with vague verbal hedges, which were the format most associated with lowered trust.
- (Inference) Avoid causal or general claims from single live examples (the press-release pattern in Sumner et al.): no "our method preserves dialect", only "here the second prompt kept X; sometimes it will not".
- (Inference) Show failures on purpose: keep a prepared example where ASR mishears dialect, or where the preservation-aware prompt overdoes it, so visitors see that the comparison is open. This is the AI-hype counter-measure in practical form (Thais), and it gives the study a reason to exist ("this is why we need a proper evaluation").
- (Inference) Label the synthetic voice as synthetic and say it is not the visitor's voice (assumption: Demo Mode uses a stock TTS voice, not voice cloning; if it clones, consent and biometric issues from Q1 apply directly).

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study specifically on communicating "study planned, no results yet" at public events; the guidance above is inferred from uncertainty-communication and exaggeration research.
- I did not find a Public Understanding of Science or Science Communication article on public reactions to machine translation errors.

## Q5. Concrete booth activities that are informative but not misleading

### Takeaway
Activities that combine prediction, comparison and conversation, run mainly on prepared examples and with nothing from visitors stored, fit both the engagement evidence and the data rules. Votes and comments can be gathered as anonymous engagement only, labelled as such, and the real study can be offered as a separate opt-in sign-up.

### Cited Findings
- Prediction before reveal improves understanding over passive watching: [Crouch et al. 2004](https://www.compadre.org/IntroPhys/items/detail.cfm?ID=2329)
- Comparison and peer judgement as a participation mechanic (up-vote/down-vote validation of clips) is used at scale in Common Voice: [Ardila et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06670)
- Citizen science principles (ECSA 2015) [official guidance from a professional association]: citizen science projects "have a genuine science outcome"; participants "receive feedback from the project", for example "how their data are being used"; citizen science is "a research approach like any other, with limitations and biases"; leaders consider legal and ethical issues including "confidentiality": [ECSA Ten Principles of Citizen Science, English PDF](https://zenodo.org/api/records/5127534/files/ECSA_Ten_Principles_of_CS_English.pdf/content)
- Separation of study participation from open play, with consent before study participation, is how Living Laboratory runs research on a public floor: [Museum of Science, Living Laboratory](https://mos.org/living-laboratory)
- Under 15, informing a parent is sufficient only when no personal data is processed, for example "observation without recording devices": [TENK 2019, section 3.3](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)

### Inferences
All of the following are inferences (booth design proposals), grounded in the findings above:
- Activity 1, "Guess what gets lost" (prediction): the researcher plays a prepared Finnish clip with dialect, slang or a culturally loaded phrase. Visitors predict which words the default translation will flatten. Reveal both translations side by side with the changed spans highlighted. Uses prepared material, so no visitor data.
- Activity 2, "Which one sounds like the speaker?" (comparison vote as engagement): on prepared examples, visitors pick A or B by tapping a counter or dropping a token. Display the running tally with a label: "Booth poll, not study data: visitors here are not a random sample and see different examples." This is honest and still gives the satisfying "what did others think" moment.
- Activity 3, "Try your own sentence" (live demo, Demo Mode): the visitor speaks; the screen shows transcript, both translations and the TTS. Sign before the microphone: "Nothing you say is saved. Audio is sent to [services] to produce the output and is not stored by us." (Assumption: verify what the third-party APIs retain and say only what is true.) Researcher offers a short prediction prompt before pressing play.
- Activity 4, "Find the error" (pipeline literacy): a poster or screen of the chain A (speech to text), B (text to English, two prompts), C (English text to synthetic speech), with one prepared example where the error enters at A and another at B. This addresses "nice, so what?" by showing why evaluating the translation separately from ASR matters.
- Activity 5, "What would you test?" (participatory framing, engagement only): visitors suggest what a fair test should check (for example dialect words, swearing, humour, politeness). Collected on paper notes with no names, explicitly as ideas for the researcher, not as data. If the researcher wants to use these ideas in the study design, acknowledging the public input in general terms is compatible with ECSA principle 8; this is not participant data.
- Activity 6, "Join the real study later" (recruitment, separate): a QR code to an information page and sign-up for the planned study, with its own privacy notice and consent. This is the only route by which a visitor becomes a research participant, and it keeps demo and study data apart.
- Avoid: recording visitors "to improve the system", claiming the vote shows which prompt is better, saying the voice is "anonymous", or using visitor sentences later as study stimuli unless consent was obtained for that before recording.
- Feedback form, if wanted: anonymous, no free text that might contain identifying details (or with a warning not to include them), questions about the booth experience only (clarity, interest), and a line saying it is event feedback, not research data. Keep it separate from any study sign-up so answers cannot be linked to contact details.
