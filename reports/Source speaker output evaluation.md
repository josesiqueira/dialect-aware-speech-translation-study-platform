# Showing speakers their AI outputs is defensible, with conditions

**Verdict: yes, the proposed order (consent, speaking tasks with no AI output, first questionnaire, review of one's own transcript and translations, second questionnaire) is scientifically defensible**, provided five conditions hold. First, the review is treated as a separate, later dataset that never edits the recordings. Second, participant ratings are read as evidence of *perceived representation* ("does this say what I meant, the way I would say it"), while the semantic accuracy of the English is also judged by blind, independent Finnish-English bilingual raters. Third, the two translation conditions are shown blinded, side by side, with randomized position and item order. Fourth, every participant's English proficiency is measured with an objective test before the review. Fifth, the new consent version names the review step, the external AI provider and the risk that the output misrepresents how the person speaks, and the local human sciences ethics committee is consulted before data collection starts, because under Finnish guidelines a review statement cannot be obtained afterwards. The design is not a textbook method. It combines established, peer-reviewed components: stimulated recall (people review a record of their own performance right after the task), member checking and interviewee transcript review (people check how their words were rendered), own-voice evaluation of AI speech (Michel et al., FAccT 2025), and standard human evaluation methods for machine translation. I found **no peer-reviewed study in which source speakers rated speech translations of their own spontaneous speech**, so the study should state this novelty openly and cite each component. This matters because the speaker is the only possible judge of the construct that makes this study distinctive, representational adequacy, and also a weak judge of English translation accuracy. The design must capture the first without overclaiming the second.

A note on labels. Statements marked **(Assumption)** are not supported by a source in the research notes. Statements marked **(Inference)** are reasoning from the cited sources, not findings stated by them. Platform facts come from the project's own code and documents, as verified by the lead researcher.

## What the platform can and cannot show at the end of a session

Several platform facts constrain the design. The platform already runs Research Mode, in which participants never see AI output during collection, followed by a background questionnaire, a post-recording questionnaire and a debrief. Translations are produced automatically about 15 to 60 seconds after each recording (automatic speech recognition, ASR, then a processing batch), so outputs exist by the end of a session. **Only the ASR transcript (A) and the translations of A can be shown live.** The human transcripts B (dialect-preserving) and C (normalized) are written later by researchers. Two translation conditions exist, default and preservation-aware, and speech synthesis uses **one fixed stock voice ("alloy"), not a clone of the participant's voice.** The project's design documents (spec sections 74, 75 and 122) already plan source-speaker evaluation as a later phase (RQ4) with the constructs semantic_fidelity, naturalness, representational_adequacy, formality, stance_preservation, regional_association and voice_similarity, presented blinded and randomized.

Two consequences follow **(Inference)**. First, the participant evaluates the whole pipeline (ASR plus translation), not translation alone. A misrecognized word in A reaches both English versions, so ASR errors add noise but are held constant across the two conditions within an utterance. The clean test of the preservation-aware prompt on correct input has to come from expert evaluation of translations of B and C later. Second, because both conditions use the same stock voice, voice_similarity is not a condition contrast. Asking "does this voice sound like you?" for every item would measure only the obvious fact that a stranger's voice is not one's own. Adding steps 4 and 5 is a protocol change that needs a new consent version and probably a new protocol version.

## Four established method families cover the design, but not in this combination

### Stimulated recall supplies the setup and the timing

Stimulated recall is "a family of introspective research procedures" in which participants are prompted by a recording of an event to recall their thinking during it ([Lyle 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/0141192032000137349)). The standard methods book in second language research stresses that **the interval between the event and the recall should be as short as possible** (Gass and Mackey 2000, contents as reported in a [LINGUIST List review](https://linguistlist.org/issues/11/1414/); the book itself was not read). A peer-reviewed example has native speakers review a video of their own interaction "immediately after the task" ([Polio, Gass and Chapin 2006](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263106060116)). The proposed step matches on three points: the participant's own recording is the stimulus, the review follows the task, and it happens in the same sitting. It differs on what is judged. Classic stimulated recall asks "what were you thinking?". This study asks "does this AI output represent what you meant and how you speak?". The "what I meant" question relies on recall of intent, so the main threats the literature names, inaccurate recall and reports that change the process being reported, apply to it directly ([Lyle 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/0141192032000137349)).

### Member checking justifies the purpose, and warns about side effects

Returning data to participants to check "for accuracy and resonance with their experiences" is called member checking or respondent validation ([Birt et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732316654870)). A review of published member checks found **"little evidence that member checks improved research findings"** when used to validate theory, but identified "representation" as one of four legitimate purposes ([Thomas 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/14780887.2016.1219435)). This study's question is a representation question, so it falls within the defensible use **(Inference)**. The closest analogue, interviewee transcript review across 51 interviews, "added little to the accuracy of the transcript" and risked "the loss of data when an interviewee chooses to remove valuable material" ([Hagens, Dobrow and Chafe 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-9-47)). People reading their own transcripts made "corrections of language" and reported "feelings of embarrassment and threat" ([Mero-Jaffe 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/160940691101000304)). For Finnish dialect speakers this predicts a specific bias: some will prefer a standardized transcript or translation because it "reads better", even when it erases their dialect **(Inference)**.

### Own-voice and own-speech AI studies give the closest precedents

The closest peer-reviewed precedent is Michel et al. (FAccT 2025). **26 interviewees heard their original recording, then AI clones of their own voice, and rated accent mimicry on a 5-point multi-item scale (Cronbach's alpha 0.883) plus overall accuracy from 1 to 100%**, followed by questions on emotional responses. Participants said some clones "neutralized" their accent, and the title quote, "It's not a representation of me", names the same construct this study targets ([Michel et al. 2025, arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1)). For text translation of one's own words into a language one does not fully command, back-translation feedback **raised users' confidence without improving objective translation quality** ([Zouhar et al. 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.14/)). That is a direct warning for the English side of this study. In a lab study of speech translation, a transcript of one's own utterance was the main way speakers could tell whether they had been recognized, and users rated transcription errors only when a transcript was shown, because otherwise "participants had no mean to assess the speech recognition quality" ([Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf)). In machine translation-mediated chat, users treated fluency as a "(misleading) proxy for adequacy" ([Robertson and Díaz 2022](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf)).

### Perceptual dialectology supports asking "does this sound like me?", but not "which features do I use?"

Finnish folk linguistics routinely asks speakers about their own dialect ([Mielikäinen and Palander 2002](https://journal.fi/sananjalka/article/view/86635); [Palander 2007](https://journal.fi/virittaja/article/download/40541/9967/28049)), so the "how I speak" question has disciplinary precedent. Sociolinguistics also shows that **conscious self-reports of one's own usage are often inaccurate** and shaped by prestige ([Trudgill 1972](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0047404500000488)). The Finnish evidence points in both directions. North Karelian speakers know their dialect is seen as "broad", do not find these features attractive, and associate them with a rural stigma, while South Karelians see their own speech as neutral and close to standard Finnish ([Palander 2007](https://journal.fi/virittaja/article/download/40541/9967/28049)). Finns' attitudes to their own speech are usually ambivalent, a conflict between status and solidarity ([Mielikäinen and Palander 2002](https://journal.fi/sananjalka/article/view/86635)). So some speakers will welcome standardized output and others will penalize it. Perceived representation is valid data about perception and identity. Actual preservation of dialect features must be measured separately by annotation, following Labov's principle that agreement is "best reached by convergence of several kinds of data with complementary sources of error" ([Labov 1972](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0047404500006576)).

## The proposed order protects the speech data, and the literature supports it

Placing the reveal after all speaking tasks is the most important design decision, and the proposed order gets it right. **Speakers change how they talk once they see a machine mishandle their speech.** In a speech translation lab study, 84.8% of participants said they adapted their speech (slower, clearer, simpler words), and self-rated recognition quality rose across rounds while actual transcription stayed "far from perfect" ([Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf)). In a diary study, **93% of African American participants modified their speech after ASR errors**, including avoiding slang ([Mengesha et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002)). Black older adults described dropping informal expressions as "kind of like code-switching" ([Harrington et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9307214/)). Showing output between tasks would push speakers toward standard Finnish and contaminate the dialectal and colloquial speech the study depends on **(Inference)**. No source names "delay AI feedback until after elicitation" as a formal procedure, so this rationale is built from these separate findings.

Putting the first questionnaire before the reveal is equally sound. Answering one question makes related information more accessible and pulls later answers toward it ([Strack, Martin and Schwarz 1988](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1002/ejsp.2420180505)). A participant's self-description of their own speech, dialect identity and attitudes to AI translation, collected after reading the translations, would no longer be an independent baseline **(Inference)**. A comparable machine translation study asked about familiarity with and opinion of machine translation before showing any output "to establish a baseline trust level" ([Martindale and Carpuat 2018](https://aclanthology.org/W18-1803.pdf)). A review session placed after the tasks need not change later performance: learners who did stimulated recall showed no significant difference on later tests from controls ([Egi 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658410802146859)). Here no speech follows the review, so this risk is close to zero. One reactivity point remains inside the review itself. Asking people to **explain** their thinking changes performance, while plain reporting does not ([Fox, Ericsson and Best 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021663)), so "why?" questions belong after the ratings, not before them **(Inference)**.

## Who can judge what: the speaker owns intent and identity, not English accuracy

The design must give each construct to the judge who can actually assess it. Raters who cannot check meaning respond to fluency more than to adequacy ([Martindale and Carpuat 2018](https://aclanthology.org/W18-1803/); [Robertson and Díaz 2022](https://facctconference.org/static/pdfs_2022/facct22-3534638.pdf)). Professional translators agree with each other more and discriminate better than non-experts ([Toral et al. 2018](https://aclanthology.org/W18-6312/)), and expert error annotation (MQM) ranks systems differently from crowd raters ([Freitag et al. 2021](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1162/tacl_a_00437)). **LexTALE, a 5-minute English vocabulary test, predicted proficiency better than self-ratings** ([Lemhöfer and Broersma 2012](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3758/s13428-011-0146-0)). The allocation below is a recommendation **(Inference)** built on these sources.

| Construct (spec name) | What the participant is asked | Valid judge | Second judge needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASR transcript accuracy (new, not in spec list) | "Is this what you said?" and mark wrong words, while hearing own audio | Participant (own language, own words) | Researcher reference transcript, for word error rate |
| representational_adequacy (primary outcome) | "How well does this version represent what you said and how you said it?" | Participant only: nobody else knows their intended self-presentation | None possible; this is why the in-session design exists |
| semantic_fidelity | "Does this version say what you meant?" | Participant, valid only with enough English; moderated by LexTALE score | Yes: blind Finnish-English bilingual raters (MQM or ESA style) |
| formality | Relative bipolar scale: "much more informal than I said it ... the same ... much more formal" | Participant, with English proficiency as moderator | Expert rating of English register |
| stance_preservation | Relative scale: "much less certain / emphatic ... the same ... much more" | Participant, with proficiency as moderator | Expert rating |
| naturalness | "How natural does this English sound?" | Fluent English readers | Yes; participant ratings at low proficiency are noisy |
| regional_association | Rephrased: "Does this version sound like it comes from a particular place or group?" | Participant cannot judge English regional varieties reliably | Expert or English-native raters for the English side |
| voice_similarity | Not asked per item (same stock voice in both conditions) | Not meaningful with a stock voice | Reserve for a future phase only if voice cloning is ever used |

The relative scales anchored on "the same as I said it" for formality and stance are a design choice **(Inference)**. They express each construct as representation rather than as an absolute property, and they avoid leading wording such as "is it too formal?". Error annotation such as MQM needs bilingual expertise ([Freitag et al. 2021](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1162/tacl_a_00437)); Error Span Annotation reaches MQM-level quality without MQM experts ([Kocmi et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.131/)). Both suit the later expert pass, not the participant session.

## Side-by-side, blinded presentation gives the most reliable comparison

### Scales and presentation

The core contrast is two outputs of the same source, which is where comparative methods are strongest. In the 2007 machine translation evaluation campaign, **inter-annotator kappa for 5-point absolute fluency and adequacy was only .250 and .226, against .373 for sentence ranking** ([Callison-Burch et al. 2007](https://aclanthology.org/W07-0718/)). Side-by-side evaluation reached higher agreement than rating each output alone and revealed subtle errors ([Song et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1002/)). Relative assessment of several outputs at once improved reliability ([Novikova, Dušek and Rieser 2018](https://aclanthology.org/N18-2012/)), and best-worst choices beat rating scales ([Kiritchenko and Mohammad 2017](https://aclanthology.org/P17-2074/)). A continuous scale was as valid as a 7-point scale and found more significant differences ([Belz and Kow 2011](https://aclanthology.org/P11-2040/)). Direct Assessment uses a 100-point slider with per-rater z-scores and quality check items ([Graham et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462)). Changing only the scale increment or the instruction wording changed Mean Opinion Scores for some speech synthesis systems ([Kirkland et al. 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/ssw_2023/kirkland23_ssw.html)).

The recommended screen is a hybrid **(Inference)**. Show both English versions of one utterance on one screen, labelled only "Version 1" and "Version 2", with left or right position randomized per item and logged. Rate each version on each construct, then ask one forced-choice question: "Which version better represents how you said it?". Fix and report the exact wording, labels and increments of every scale, and never merge constructs into "overall quality".

### Blinding, randomization and quality checks

Within-subject designs are efficient but exposed to practice, sensitization and carryover ([Greenwald 1976](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0033-2909.83.2.314)). The controls below are recommendations **(Inference)**. Never show the words "default", "preservation-aware", "dialect" or "improved" on screen, in visible file names or in the experimenter's speech. Randomize utterance order per participant. Run the review as self-paced software with scripted instructions, and keep the experimenter unaware of which side holds which condition. A participant can learn after a few items that one version is "the informal one", so include trial number by condition in the analysis to test whether the gap grows. Embed one quality check item in which one version has a deliberately changed meaning, following Direct Assessment's degraded items ([Graham et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462)). It doubles as a per-person check of whether the participant can detect meaning errors in English. Mention in consent that "some versions are included as checks" and explain them in the debrief.

### Fatigue and number of items

Later questions in a web survey were answered faster, with shorter open answers and less varied grid answers ([Galesic and Bosnjak 2009](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/poq/nfp031)). Six constructs times two versions times 20 utterances is 240 ratings, too many for one sitting **(Inference)**. No source gives a validated maximum, so the cap must come from piloting. A starting point of 8 to 12 utterances per participant, sampled across tasks, is an **(Assumption)** to test in the pilot. Statistical power depends on both participants and items ([Brysbaert and Stevens 2018](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.5334/joc.10)), and a non-significant difference from a low-powered test can be a missed effect rather than a tie ([Graham, Haddow and Koehn 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.6/)). Set the sample size by simulation from pilot data.

### Synthesized audio goes last, beside the original

Familiar listeners rated clones of their own voice differently from unfamiliar listeners ([Rosi, Soopramanien and McGettigan 2025](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10206954/1/1-s2.0-S2949882125000271-main.pdf)). Hearing the original speaker's voice alongside the synthetic voice was valued in a speech translation study: "if you only get the robotic voice, I think it just cuts you off from the person" ([Hara and Iqbal 2015](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/pn1497-haraA.pdf)). Present audio in a separate block after all text ratings, so voice quality cannot spill into fidelity or representation ratings, and play the participant's own recording first **(Inference)**. Tell participants plainly that the English voice is a stock synthetic voice, not theirs. Ask once per participant how acceptable it is for that voice to speak their words, not per item.

## Analysis: ordinal mixed models, a pre-registered primary outcome, and triangulation

Treating ordinal ratings as metric can produce false alarms, lost power and even reversed effects, and averaging items does not fix it ([Liddell and Kruschke 2018](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.08.009)). Ordinal models fitted in brms give better inference for such data ([Bürkner and Vuorre 2019](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/2515245918823199)). Confirmatory mixed models should use the maximal random-effects structure the design justifies ([Barr et al. 2013](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jml.2012.11.001)).

Each rating is indexed by participant, utterance, version and construct. **Utterances are nested within participants**, because each person rates their own speech. The shared speaking task or prompt is crossed with participants **(Inference)**. A model per construct, as a cumulative link mixed model, would be: rating ~ condition * proficiency + position + trial + asr_error * condition + (1 + condition | participant) + (1 + condition | prompt) + (1 | participant:utterance). It can be fitted with brms (family cumulative) or the R package ordinal (function clmm); the ordinal package documentation was not checked in this research. If a 100-point slider is used, a linear mixed model on per-participant z-scores follows Direct Assessment practice ([Graham et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/natural-language-engineering/article/can-machine-translation-systems-be-evaluated-by-the-crowd-alone/E29DA2BC8E6B99AA1481CC92FAB58462)). The forced choice can be analysed with a mixed logistic model, with position as a covariate for side bias.

Pre-register **representational_adequacy as the primary outcome** of this phase, and correct for multiple testing across the other constructs **(Inference)**. Then use the design's real strength, convergence of data with different sources of error ([Labov 1972](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0047404500006576)). Compare participant and expert fidelity ratings item by item. The pre-registered prediction from the fluency mechanism is that, at lower proficiency, participants' fidelity ratings track their naturalness ratings more closely. Keep participants' transcript corrections as their own dataset and never use them to overwrite reference transcripts B and C, which avoids the "inconsistent data sources" problem ([Hagens et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-9-47)). If researchers do consult the corrections, log it.

## Ethics: probably no mandatory review in Finland, but ask before starting

Under TENK's guidelines, an ethical review statement is mandatory only when research deviates from informed consent, intervenes physically, involves minors under 15 without parental consent or information, uses "exceptionally strong stimuli", risks "mental harm that exceeds the limits of normal daily life", or threatens safety ([TENK 2019, section 4.2](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)). Research may include "mental strain and emotional experiences similar to situations of everyday life". Seeing a misrecognized transcript of one's own speech is likely within that range, since auto-captions and voice typing are everyday experiences **(Inference, not a ruling)**. The judgment would change if consent did not mention the review (criterion a), if outputs could be mocking or stereotyping, or if voice cloning were added. Because the statement cannot be obtained afterwards, and because local committees can make the criteria more specific (not checked for the host university), the safe course is to ask before the new protocol version starts. **(Assumption)**: participants recorded under the current consent version have not agreed to see their outputs, so they should not be shown them unless they re-consent.

The documented harms are real but come mostly from repeated everyday use. ASR errors caused frustration in 77% of diary participants, and **17% blamed themselves for how they spoke** ([Mengesha et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664002)). Black participants exposed to high error rates showed more self-consciousness and lower self-esteem ([Wenzel et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581357)). In the one-session own-voice study, no participant reported distress and none withdrew ([Michel et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09346v1)). The racialized US context differs from Finnish regional variation, so how strongly these harms transfer is an **(Assumption)**. Recognition of colloquial Finnish is a known weak point: models trained on formal Finnish parliament speech do not generalize to colloquial speech ([Moisio et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)). Errors will therefore be common, which supports framing the review as an evaluation of the system and saying so in advance.

On data protection, research consent is "not the same thing as consent which is used as a legal basis for processing personal data", and the data controller chooses the legal basis ([TENK 2019](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)). In Finland, public interest under section 4(1) of the Data Protection Act is widely used, alongside a separate consent to participate ([University of Helsinki statement 2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/consultation_feedback/1417/feedback_1417_statement_uh_edpb_data-protection-in-research.pdf); statute text not read). The EDPB states that **"voice data is inherently biometric personal data"** and that anonymizing voice is "specially challenging" ([EDPB Guidelines 02/2021, paras 31 and 105](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)). That guidance concerns voice assistants, so applying it to research is by analogy. The privacy notice must list recipients and third-country transfers ([TENK 2019](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)), so the notice must name OpenAI as a recipient of recordings for ASR, translation and speech synthesis. OpenAI's retention policy and processing location were not verified in this research and must be checked with the data protection officer before any claim is made. Dialect itself can point to a small community, so quoting regional speech in publications carries a re-identification risk **(Inference)**.

## Booth plan for 23 October 2026: engaging, honest, and no research data

### The honest default is that the booth collects nothing

Using visitors' speech or answers as research data would need, before the event, a research plan purpose, a named controller, a legal basis, a privacy notice at the point of collection, a consent-to-participate step and a withdrawal route ([TENK 2019](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)). The booth is 14 days away, so **the honest default is that the booth produces no research data** **(Inference)**. Both large public speech campaigns built information into the app before any recording: Lahjoita puhetta donors "had to acknowledge that they were informed of the conditions of participation before they could start donating", under legitimate interest with a data protection impact assessment ([Moisio et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9362968/)). Common Voice used explicit online donation and released only a pseudonymous speaker ID ([Ardila et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06670)).

Two platform settings decide what the sign may truthfully say. **demoPersist is TRUE by default in the seed, so Demo Mode currently keeps recordings and outputs until a reset.** Set it to false and test that a demo session is actually deleted when it ends. Even then, audio goes to OpenAI for ASR, translation (with store:false) and speech synthesis, and the provider's retention was not verified. Until it is, the sign must not say that nothing is kept anywhere. Under-15 visitors speaking into the app would produce minors' voice data, which is one more reason to keep nothing ([TENK 2019, section 3.3](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)).

Draft sign: "This is a demo, not a study result. We do not keep your recording or the translations. To produce them, your audio is sent to OpenAI's service. The English voice is a computer voice, not yours. We are planning a study that will test whether the second translation style keeps more of how people speak. One example cannot tell us which is better." **(Assumption)**: the OpenAI sentence must be finalized after the retention check, including where the processing happens. Stating uncertainty concretely costs little trust: across five experiments (n = 5,780), communicating uncertainty produced "only a small decrease in trust", mostly for vague verbal statements ([van der Bles et al. 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1073/pnas.1913678117&resultType=core&format=json)). Exaggeration mostly starts with researchers, and caveats did not reduce news uptake ([Sumner et al. 2014](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1136/bmj.g7015&resultType=core&format=json)).

### Activities

Students who predicted a demonstration's outcome before seeing it understood it significantly better, while passive watchers understood it no better than people who saw nothing ([Crouch et al. 2004](https://www.compadre.org/IntroPhys/items/detail.cfm?ID=2329)). That finding comes from classroom physics, so applying it to a booth is an **(Assumption)**. Exhibits work when they are immediately understandable and motivating at every step ([Allen 2004](https://informalscience.org/research/designs-learning-studying-science-museum-exhibits-do-more-entertain)). AI literacy exhibits benefit from personally meaningful output and collaboration ([Long, Blunt and Magerko 2021](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3476034?fields=title,venue,year,authors,abstract)).

| Activity | What the visitor does | What is kept |
|---|---|---|
| Guess what gets lost | Hears a prepared Finnish clip with dialect, slang or an idiom, predicts what the English will lose, then sees both translations side by side with changed words highlighted | Nothing (prepared material) |
| Try your own sentence | Speaks a dialect word, a family saying or slang in Demo Mode; the researcher asks for a prediction before pressing play; sees transcript, both translations, hears the stock voice | Nothing, once demoPersist is false and deletion is tested; provider retention unverified |
| Find where the error enters | Poster or screen of the chain: speech to Finnish text, text to two English versions, English text to computer voice; one prepared example where ASR fails, one where translation fails, one where the preservation-aware version overdoes it | Nothing |
| Which one sounds like the speaker? | Votes A or B on prepared examples with a token or tap counter; running tally labelled "Booth poll, not study data: visitors are not a random sample" | Anonymous counts only |
| What should a fair test check? | Writes ideas (swearing, humor, politeness, names) on unsigned notes | Ideas for the researcher, not participant data; may be acknowledged in general terms ([ECSA principles](https://zenodo.org/api/records/5127534/files/ECSA_Ten_Principles_of_CS_English.pdf/content)) |
| Join the real study later | QR code to the study's information page and sign-up | Contact details only, under their own privacy notice, kept apart from everything else |

Showing failures on purpose matters. It counters the research community's own role in AI hype ([Thais 2024](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31737)) and explains why the study needs a proper evaluation **(Inference)**. The Museum of Science "Living Laboratory" shows that open play and consented research can share a public floor, as long as study participation goes through consent first and stays separate ([Museum of Science](https://mos.org/living-laboratory)).

### What booth interactions can and cannot count as

| Booth interaction | Can it be research data? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Visitor audio, transcripts, translations | No | Personal data with no prior information, legal basis or consent; voice is hard to anonymize ([EDPB 02/2021](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-07/edpb_guidelines_202102_on_vva_v2.0_adopted_en.pdf)) |
| A/B vote tallies with no audio or free text | No, engagement only | Outside GDPR if truly anonymous ([GDPR Recital 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-26/)), but visitors were not told they were in research and the sample is self-selected |
| Anonymous booth feedback form (clarity, interest) | No, event feedback | Closer to service evaluation; keep minimal, warn against identifying details, keep apart from sign-ups |
| Ideas on sticky notes | No, design input | May inform the protocol; not participant data |
| Study sign-up via QR code | Recruitment, not data | Visitor becomes a participant only later, through the study's own consent |
| Visitor sentences reused later as study stimuli | No, unless consent covered this before recording | TENK requires information at collection |

Researchers must also avoid saying "your voice is anonymous" (pseudonymous IDs do not anonymize a voice), claiming the vote shows which prompt is better, recording "to improve the system", or promising complete anonymity, which TENK forbids when it cannot be guaranteed ([TENK 2019, section 3.6](https://tenk.fi/sites/default/files/2021-01/Ethical_review_in_human_sciences_2020.pdf)). If visitors are students of the researcher's own university, TENK's caution about dependent relationships applies to any later study participation.

## Conclusion

The proposed session is defensible because it asks the source speaker the one question only they can answer: does this output represent me? It does not ask them to certify English accuracy. The platform fact that only ASR-based outputs exist at session end turns out to be a design asset if it is named honestly. The participant evaluates the real end-to-end experience a speaker of colloquial Finnish would have. Expert evaluation of translations of B and C isolates the translation prompt's effect on clean input. Agreement or disagreement between the two becomes a finding in itself, including the gap, not yet studied, in how speakers judge ASR transcripts of their own dialectal speech. The two places where the study can still fail are outside the statistics: a consent form that does not describe the review, and a public sign that promises more privacy than the external provider has been shown to deliver. Both need fixing before the next session and before 23 October.
